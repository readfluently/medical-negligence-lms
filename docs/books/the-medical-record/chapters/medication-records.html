Chapter 4 — Medication Records

Reading prescriptions, administration records and medication decisions

1. Why Medication Records Matter

Medication records can provide important evidence about what treatment was prescribed, what treatment was administered, when it was administered, and sometimes why a particular medication was withheld or changed.

In a medical-negligence investigation, medication records may therefore help reconstruct the treatment provided to the patient and identify questions requiring further investigation.

Practitioner's Principle

Do not assume that a medication being prescribed means that it was administered, or that an administered medication was given at the prescribed time.

2. Different Medication Documents

Medication information may appear in several parts of the medical record.

  • prescriptions;
  • medication orders;
  • medication administration records;
  • nursing notes;
  • practitioner progress notes;
  • pharmacy records; and
  • discharge medication lists.

These documents should be compared rather than automatically treated as interchangeable.

3. Prescription Versus Administration

One of the most important distinctions is between an order for medication and evidence that the medication was actually administered.

A practitioner may prescribe a medication, but the administration record may show whether it was subsequently given.

Conversely, the administration record may identify a medication that was administered and provide a time that can be compared with other clinical events.

Litigation Question

What was ordered, what was actually administered, when was it administered, and what happened afterwards?

4. A Fictional Medication Administration Record

The following is a fictional reconstruction created for training purposes. It does not represent a real patient.

FICTIONAL MEDICATION ADMINISTRATION RECORD Patient: Patient A
Date: 14 March
Medication Ordered Dose Route Scheduled Time Administration Recorded By
Analgesic A As prescribed Oral 09:00 Given 09:05 Nurse 1
Antiemetic B As prescribed IV 09:15 Given 09:20 Nurse 1
Analgesic A As prescribed Oral 13:00 Not recorded —
Antiemetic B As prescribed IV 13:15 Given 13:30 Nurse 2
Notice the distinction.

The scheduled time is not necessarily the administration time.

The record also contains an entry where administration is not recorded. That creates an investigation point, but it does not automatically establish what happened.

5. Medication Timing

Timing may become important when medication is being considered in relation to the patient's clinical condition.

The practitioner should establish when the medication was ordered, when it was due, when it was administered and what clinical events occurred before and after administration.

This may require comparison with:

  • nursing observations;
  • practitioner notes;
  • laboratory results;
  • procedure records;
  • emergency or deterioration notes; and
  • discharge documentation.
Medication timing should be placed inside the clinical chronology.

6. When Medication Is Withheld

Medication records may show that a medication was not given.

The reason may sometimes be documented, such as a clinical decision, a change in the treatment plan, a contraindication, a patient's refusal or another recorded circumstance.

Where the reason is not clear, the absence of administration should become an investigation question.

Ask:

Was the medication deliberately withheld, inadvertently omitted, delayed, refused by the patient, or simply incompletely documented?

The available evidence should be examined before reaching a conclusion.

7. Medication Changes

Medication orders may change as the patient's condition changes.

A practitioner may start, stop, increase, decrease or substitute medication.

The practitioner investigating the case should identify these changes and determine where they fit in the chronology.

FICTIONAL MEDICATION CHANGE LOG Patient: Patient A

08:30

Initial order
Analgesic A commenced.

12:45

Clinical review
Patient reports continuing abdominal pain.

13:00

Medication change
Treatment plan amended.

The important point is not simply that the medication changed. The practitioner must investigate the clinical circumstances surrounding the change.

8. Interrogating Medication Records

Medication records should be examined systematically.

Question 1 — What was prescribed?

Identify the medication, dose, route and relevant timing.

Question 2 — What was administered?

Determine whether there is contemporaneous evidence that the medication was actually given.

Question 3 — When was it administered?

Compare the administration time with the prescribed or scheduled time.

Question 4 — Who administered it?

Identify the recorded person responsible for administration where the record provides that information.

Question 5 — Was anything withheld or delayed?

Identify unexplained omissions, delays or changes.

Question 6 — What happened afterwards?

Compare the medication event with the patient's symptoms, observations and subsequent clinical decisions.

9. Medication Records and the Wider Medical Record

Medication documentation should not be analysed in isolation.

A medication entry may only become meaningful when compared with other parts of the record.

Example:

If a medication was recorded as administered at 13:30, examine what the patient's condition was at that time and what happened during the following period.

The practitioner may need to compare the medication record with nursing observations, practitioner notes and subsequent treatment.

10. The Practitioner Test

Before moving to the next category of medical record, ask:

Can I distinguish between what was prescribed, what was actually administered, when it was administered, and what clinical events occurred around the medication decision?

If not, further documentary investigation is required.

Legal and Evidentiary Note

Medication records are part of the documentary evidence from which treatment may be reconstructed.

An apparent omission, delay or discrepancy should be investigated in the context of the complete record before a legal conclusion is drawn.

The existence of a medication entry, by itself, does not answer the broader question of whether the treatment provided complied with the applicable standard of care.
