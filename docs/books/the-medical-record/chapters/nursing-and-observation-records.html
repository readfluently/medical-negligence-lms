BOOK 3 — THE MEDICAL RECORD

Chapter 3 — Nursing and Observation Records

Reading bedside documentation, monitoring and changes in the patient's condition

Book Home Nursing Records Observation Chart Investigation

1. Why Nursing Records Matter

Nursing records can provide a detailed account of what happened to a patient between formal practitioner assessments.

They may record observations, symptoms reported by the patient, changes in condition, medication administration, interventions, communication with practitioners and the patient's response to treatment.

For the medical-negligence practitioner, these records may therefore provide important evidence about the patient's clinical course.

Practitioner's Principle

Do not treat nursing documentation as secondary paperwork. It may contain the most detailed contemporaneous account of how the patient's condition changed over time.

2. The Difference Between a Single Assessment and Continuous Monitoring

A practitioner may assess a patient at particular points in time.

Nursing staff may observe and document the patient repeatedly between those assessments.

This means that nursing records can help reveal changes that would not necessarily appear in a single practitioner note.

For example, a patient may initially appear relatively stable, but later develop increasing pain, changes in observations, repeated vomiting, altered consciousness or other concerning features.

The significance of those changes depends on the circumstances of the case and the clinical evidence.

3. A Fictional Nursing Record

The following is a fictional reconstruction created for training purposes. It does not represent a real patient.

FICTIONAL NURSING PROGRESS RECORD Patient: Patient A
Date: 14 March

08:10 — Initial Nursing Assessment

Patient's report
Reports abdominal pain and nausea.
Appearance
Awake and communicating appropriately.
Action
Patient settled in treatment area and practitioner notified.

10:15 — Nursing Review

Patient's report
Continues to report abdominal pain.
Action
Patient monitored. Further assessment requested.

11:45 — Change in Condition

Patient's report
Reports increasing abdominal pain and weakness.
Appearance
Appears uncomfortable.
Action
Practitioner informed.

13:20 — Further Review

Observation
Patient remains uncomfortable.
Action
Continued monitoring documented.
Notice the chronology.

The nursing record provides several observations over time. It may therefore reveal a developing pattern rather than simply a single clinical snapshot.

4. The Observation Chart

Observation charts are particularly important because they may record measurable changes in a patient's condition.

Depending on the clinical setting, an observation chart may include temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, level of consciousness and other relevant observations.

FICTIONAL OBSERVATION CHART Patient: Patient A
Date: 14 March
Time Temperature Pulse Respiratory Rate Blood Pressure Oxygen Saturation
08:00 37.9 °C 104 18 108/68 97%
10:00 38.1 °C 108 19 106/66 97%
12:00 38.4 °C 116 22 102/64 96%
14:00 38.7 °C 122 24 98/60 95%
Important:

A table of observations does not, by itself, establish negligence.

The practitioner must determine the clinical significance of the observations, what was known at the relevant time, what response occurred, and what the applicable standard of care required in the circumstances.

5. Trends Matter

One isolated observation may have limited significance.

A series of observations may reveal a trend.

The practitioner should therefore avoid reading observation values independently of the surrounding chronology.

Ask:

  • Did the patient's condition remain stable?
  • Did the observations change?
  • How quickly did they change?
  • Was the change communicated?
  • Was the patient reassessed?
  • Was treatment changed?
The evidentiary significance may lie in the pattern, not in a single number.

6. Nursing Notes and Communication

Nursing documentation may also record communication between members of the healthcare team.

Entries may indicate that a practitioner was informed of a patient's deterioration or that further review was requested.

Such entries should be examined carefully.

Ask:

What was communicated, when was it communicated, to whom, and what happened afterwards?

The answer may require comparison with practitioner notes, medication records, observation charts and other documents.

7. Interrogating Nursing and Observation Records

The practitioner should move beyond simply extracting information from the nursing records.

The records should be interrogated for their evidentiary significance.

Question 1 — Change

What changed in the patient's condition?

Question 2 — Timing

When did the change occur?

Question 3 — Recognition

When was the change recognised and documented?

Question 4 — Communication

Was the change communicated to the responsible practitioner?

Question 5 — Response

What clinical response followed?

Question 6 — Delay

Is there an unexplained period between recognition, communication and response?

Question 7 — Consistency

Do the nursing records correspond with the practitioner notes and other clinical documents?

8. Silence and Missing Entries

A medical record may contain periods where documentation is sparse or apparently absent.

That absence should be identified, but it should not automatically be converted into a factual conclusion.

The practitioner should ask whether another document may explain the apparent gap.

For example, relevant information may appear in another nursing record, a medication administration record, a practitioner note, an observation chart or a separate clinical system.

Practitioner's Caution

An undocumented event and an event that did not occur are not necessarily the same proposition.

9. Building the Chronology From Nursing Records

Nursing and observation records should be incorporated into the wider medical chronology.

Capture:
  • Time of observation
  • Patient's reported symptoms
  • Objective observations
  • Changes in condition
  • Nursing interventions
  • Communication with practitioners
  • Practitioner response
  • Subsequent observations

10. The Practitioner Test

Before moving to the next category of medical record, ask:

Have I identified how the patient's condition changed over time, who knew about the change, when they knew about it, and what response followed?

If not, the nursing and observation records have not yet been fully interrogated.

Legal and Evidentiary Note

Nursing and observation records form part of the documentary evidence from which the patient's clinical course may be reconstructed.

Their evidentiary significance must be assessed in the context of the complete record and the other evidence available in the matter.

The practitioner should distinguish carefully between what is expressly documented, what can reasonably be inferred, and what requires further evidence.
