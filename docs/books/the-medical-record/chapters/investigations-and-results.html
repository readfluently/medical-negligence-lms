Chapter 6 — Investigations and Results

Investigations are often treated as objective parts of the medical record. Laboratory results, imaging reports, pathology findings and other diagnostic tests can appear to speak for themselves.

But a litigator should not read an investigation in isolation. The important questions include why the investigation was ordered, when it was ordered, when the result became available, who received the result, what the result showed, and what happened afterwards.

Central principle: A test result is not the end of the clinical story. It is an event within the clinical timeline.

Why Investigations Matter

Investigations can provide evidence about what was happening inside the patient at a particular point in time.

They may help establish whether a condition was present, whether the patient's condition was changing, and whether information became available that should have influenced subsequent clinical decision-making.

From a litigation perspective, however, the result itself is only one part of the enquiry.

  • What prompted the investigation?
  • When was it requested?
  • When was the specimen taken or examination performed?
  • When was the result available?
  • Was the result communicated to the treating practitioner?
  • Was the result acted upon?
  • What happened after the result became known?

The Request and the Result Are Different Events

One of the most important distinctions is between the time an investigation is requested and the time the result becomes available.

A practitioner cannot respond to information that was not yet available. Conversely, once a significant result has become available, the litigation enquiry may turn to what happened after that point.

This means that the chronology should distinguish between:

  • the decision to investigate;
  • the performance of the investigation;
  • the availability of the result;
  • the communication or review of the result; and
  • the subsequent clinical decision.
Litigation principle: Do not place a result into the chronology merely at the time the test was ordered. Establish when the information actually became available.

Reading the Result in Context

A result should be read against the surrounding clinical information.

For example, an abnormal laboratory result may have greater significance when considered together with the patient's symptoms, vital signs, examination findings and previous results.

The practitioner should therefore resist the temptation to begin with the result and work backwards from the eventual outcome.

Instead, reconstruct what information was available at each decision point.

A Diagnostic Investigation Is Part of a Sequence

Clinical sequence:

Patient presents → symptoms assessed → investigation requested → specimen or examination performed → result generated → result becomes available → result reviewed → clinical decision made.

The sequence matters because a negligence enquiry is concerned with what should reasonably have been done at the relevant time.

A result generated later cannot simply be projected backwards into an earlier clinical decision.

Who Saw the Result?

The record may contain several indications that a result was reviewed. These may include practitioner notes, electronic acknowledgements, telephone communications, progress entries, amended treatment plans or references to the result in later notes.

The absence of an obvious acknowledgement does not automatically establish that nobody saw the result. It does, however, create a question for investigation.

Questions for the Practitioner

  • Who was responsible for reviewing the result?
  • When did the result become available?
  • Was it communicated to the treating team?
  • Is there evidence that it was reviewed?
  • Was the result recorded in a subsequent clinical note?
  • Did the treatment plan change afterwards?
  • If it did not change, is there a documented reason?

Investigations and Hindsight

One of the dangers when reviewing medical negligence claims is hindsight bias.

Once the eventual diagnosis or injury is known, earlier findings may appear more obvious than they were at the time.

The practitioner should therefore separate two questions:

  1. What did the investigation actually show?
  2. What significance could reasonably have been attached to that finding at the relevant time?

The second question requires the clinical context and, where appropriate, expert evidence.

Comparing Investigations with the Rest of the Record

Investigations should be compared against the admission record, observation chart, nursing notes, medication records and practitioner notes.

This may reveal important relationships.

  • An abnormal result may coincide with deterioration in vital signs.
  • A result may be available before a practitioner documents a review.
  • A treatment change may occur shortly after a result becomes available.
  • A later note may refer to a result that is not obvious in an earlier section of the file.

These connections help turn a collection of documents into a coherent clinical chronology.

The Missing Result

Sometimes the record refers to an investigation without containing the underlying report.

That gap should be identified rather than silently filled.

The practitioner should determine whether the missing document exists elsewhere, whether it was generated by another institution, whether it was communicated verbally, or whether it was never obtained.

Practical rule: A missing investigation is itself a record issue that may require investigation. Do not manufacture certainty from an incomplete file.

Practitioner Test

Before Moving On, Ask:

  • Have all significant investigations been identified?
  • Do the dates and times make sense?
  • When did each result become available?
  • Who appears to have reviewed the result?
  • What action followed the result?
  • Are there unexplained gaps?
  • Are there results referred to elsewhere but missing from the file?
  • Have I avoided hindsight reasoning?

Legal and Evidentiary Note

Investigations can become important evidence when assessing whether a practitioner acted reasonably in the circumstances existing at the relevant time.

The practitioner should distinguish between the factual evidence contained in the record and the conclusions that may ultimately require expert interpretation.

A laboratory or imaging result may establish what the investigation showed. It does not, by itself, necessarily establish what the appropriate clinical response was.

That distinction becomes particularly important when the litigation question moves from what happened to whether the response met the applicable standard of care.
