Chapter 7 — Procedures, Operations and Anaesthetic Records

Procedures and operations generate some of the most important records in a medical negligence investigation. They may contain information about what was planned, what was actually performed, who participated, what was found, what difficulties occurred and how the patient was managed afterwards.

Anaesthetic records provide another layer of information. They may document the patient's condition before the procedure, medication and monitoring during anaesthesia, physiological changes and events occurring during the operation.

Central principle: Do not treat the operation note as the entire account of the procedure. Reconstruct the procedure by comparing the operation, anaesthetic, nursing, medication and post-operative records.

Why Procedure Records Matter

A procedure record can answer questions that cannot be answered from the admission record alone.

  • What procedure was intended?
  • Why was it performed?
  • When did it begin and end?
  • Who performed or assisted with the procedure?
  • What was found?
  • Were there complications or unexpected findings?
  • What treatment was provided afterwards?

The record may also reveal a difference between what was planned and what actually occurred.

A Fictional Operation Record

Planned Procedure Versus Actual Procedure

The planned procedure may be documented in a booking form, consent documentation or pre-operative note.

The actual procedure is usually documented in the operation or procedure note.

These documents should be compared.

A difference between the planned and actual procedure is not automatically evidence of negligence. Clinical circumstances may require a practitioner to modify the procedure. The important question is why the difference occurred and whether the relevant decision was clinically justified and appropriately documented.

Practical rule: Record the difference first. Decide its legal significance only after understanding the clinical circumstances.

The anaesthetic record can provide a detailed timeline of the patient's physiological condition during a procedure.

Do Not Read the Anaesthetic Record in Isolation

An anaesthetic chart may show a physiological change without explaining everything that caused it.

The investigator should therefore compare it with:

  • the operation note;
  • the medication administration record;
  • the nursing record;
  • the observation record;
  • the recovery-room record; and
  • any incident or complication documentation.

This comparison may reveal whether the same event appears consistently across different records.

Unexpected Events During the Procedure

When an unexpected event occurs, the practitioner should identify the earliest point at which the event appears in the record.

Then ask:

  • What happened?
  • When did it happen?
  • Who was present?
  • What was immediately observed?
  • What intervention followed?
  • Did the patient's condition change?
  • Was the event documented consistently elsewhere?

The Post-Operative Record

The procedure does not end for litigation purposes when the operation note is signed.

The post-operative period may be critical.

Look for evidence of the patient's condition immediately after the procedure and during the hours that followed.

  • Recovery-room observations
  • Pain and medication records
  • Vital signs
  • Nursing observations
  • Practitioner review
  • Post-operative investigations
  • New symptoms or complications

A complication identified after surgery may therefore require the investigator to look backwards as well as forwards through the record.

Timing Is Critical

Procedure records often contain several different times:

  • time of transfer to theatre;
  • time anaesthesia commenced;
  • time the procedure commenced;
  • time an important event occurred;
  • time the procedure ended; and
  • time the patient entered recovery.

These should not automatically be treated as interchangeable.

Chronology example:

15:10 — theatre transfer → 15:25 — anaesthesia → 15:45 — procedure begins → 16:10 — physiological change → 16:20 — condition improves → 17:00 — procedure completed.

This sequence may be more informative than any individual document.

Comparing Different Accounts of the Procedure

It is common for the same clinical episode to appear in several records.

The operation note may describe the surgical findings. The anaesthetic record may document physiological changes. Nursing notes may describe observations and communications. Medication records may establish when particular drugs were administered.

The investigator should compare these accounts rather than assuming that the most detailed document is necessarily complete.

Cross-Record Questions

  • Do the recorded times correspond?
  • Is the same complication described consistently?
  • Does the medication record support the procedure note?
  • Do nursing observations support the anaesthetic record?
  • Are there unexplained differences?
  • Is there a document that appears to be missing?

Documentation After a Complication

Where a complication is alleged, the record should be examined carefully for contemporaneous documentation.

The investigator should identify what was recorded at the time, rather than relying only on later summaries prepared after the outcome was known.

Later accounts may still be relevant, but they should be distinguished from contemporaneous records when reconstructing the clinical sequence.

Practitioner Test

Before Moving On, Ask:

  • Have I obtained the operation or procedure record?
  • Have I obtained the anaesthetic record where relevant?
  • Have I identified the planned and actual procedure?
  • Have I reconstructed the procedural timeline?
  • Have I identified unexpected events?
  • Have I compared the operation and anaesthetic records?
  • Have I checked the post-operative records?
  • Have I identified unexplained gaps or inconsistencies?

Legal and Evidentiary Note

Procedure and anaesthetic records can provide important contemporaneous evidence about what occurred during treatment.

They may assist in establishing the factual sequence, but the existence of an adverse event does not by itself establish negligence.

The legal enquiry still requires the practitioner to distinguish between the factual record and the clinical and legal conclusions that may need to be drawn from it.

In particular, an unexpected outcome should not automatically be equated with a breach of the applicable standard of care.

Remember: The purpose of reconstructing the procedure is first to establish what happened. Only then should the investigation move to why it happened and whether the response was reasonable in the circumstances.
