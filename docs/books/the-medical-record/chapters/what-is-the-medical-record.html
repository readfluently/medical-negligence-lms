Understanding the record before learning how to interrogate it

1. The Medical Record Is More Than a File

A medical record is not simply a collection of pages kept by a hospital or practitioner.

For the medical-negligence practitioner, it can provide a chronological documentary account of the patient's clinical journey and the decisions made during that journey.

It may contain evidence about the patient's condition, assessments, investigations, treatment, monitoring, communication, referrals, procedures and discharge.

Practitioner's Principle

Read the medical record as evidence of events and decisions, not merely as administrative paperwork.

2. What Can the Record Tell You?

Different parts of a medical record may answer different questions.

  • What was the patient's condition when care began?
  • What symptoms or complaints were recorded?
  • What assessments were performed?
  • What investigations were requested?
  • What treatment was provided?
  • What changes occurred in the patient's condition?
  • What decisions were made in response?
  • When were those decisions made?

3. A Simple Record Example

The following is a fictional reconstruction created for training purposes. It does not represent a real patient.

FICTIONAL CLINICAL RECORD Patient: Patient A
Date: 14 March
08:00
Patient presents with abdominal pain and vomiting.
08:20
Initial clinical assessment documented.
09:00
Blood tests requested.
11:30
Patient reports increasing abdominal pain.
12:15
Further clinical review documented.
14:00
Patient transferred for further assessment.
Notice what this record gives us.

It gives us times, events and documented decisions. It begins to establish a chronology.

But it does not necessarily answer every question.

4. What the Record Does Not Automatically Tell You

A medical record may document an event without explaining everything that happened around it.

An entry stating that a patient was reviewed does not necessarily tell us everything that was considered during that review.

Similarly, the absence of a documented event does not automatically establish that the event did not occur.

The practitioner must therefore distinguish between:

  • what the record expressly records;
  • what can reasonably be inferred from the record;
  • what remains uncertain; and
  • what requires confirmation from another source.

5. Interrogating the Record

The purpose of reading the record is not merely to summarise it. The practitioner must begin asking questions.

Question 1 — Timing

What happened first, and how much time passed before the next clinical decision?

Question 2 — Knowledge

What information appears to have been available to the practitioner at the relevant time?

Question 3 — Response

What action followed the patient's symptoms, observations or investigation results?

Question 4 — Gaps

Are there periods where the record becomes silent despite clinically significant events?

Question 5 — Consistency

Does the record agree with the patient's account, other documents and later evidence?

6. The Record and the Patient's Story

The medical record should not automatically replace the patient's account.

Nor should the patient's account automatically override the documentary record.

The practitioner should compare the different sources of information and identify where they agree, where they conflict, and what further evidence may resolve the conflict.

The record is one part of the evidentiary story.

Its value increases when it is placed alongside the patient's account, witness evidence, expert evidence and other documents.

7. The Beginning of the Medical Chronology

One of the first practical uses of the medical record is to construct a chronology.

At this stage, the chronology should remain factual. The practitioner should resist the temptation to insert the legal conclusion before the underlying events have been tested.

Record each significant event:
  • Date and time
  • Patient's condition
  • Relevant observation or investigation
  • Practitioner's assessment
  • Decision made
  • Action taken
  • Change in condition
  • Next clinical response

8. The Practitioner Test

Before moving to the individual components of the medical record, ask:

Can I reconstruct what happened from the documents I have, identify what remains uncertain, and explain what additional evidence I need?

If the answer is no, the investigation is not yet complete.

Legal and Evidentiary Note

The medical record should be approached as documentary evidence within the broader evidentiary framework of the case. Its contents must be considered together with the surrounding evidence and the legal questions that ultimately have to be proved.

Later chapters will examine particular categories of records and the questions that arise from them.
