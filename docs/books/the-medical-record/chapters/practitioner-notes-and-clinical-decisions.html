BOOK 3 — THE MEDICAL RECORD

Chapter 5 — Practitioner Notes and Clinical Decisions

Reading the practitioner's reasoning, assessment and response through the clinical record

Book Home Practitioner Notes Clinical Decisions Investigation

1. Why Practitioner Notes Matter

Practitioner notes may provide evidence of the assessment made, information considered, treatment selected and decisions taken during the patient's care.

They may also help identify what the practitioner knew at a particular point in time and what clinical response followed.

For the medical-negligence practitioner, the note is therefore more than a summary of the patient's condition.

Practitioner's Principle

Read the practitioner's note for both the clinical facts it records and the decisions it reveals.

2. The Difference Between a Note and a Decision

A practitioner may record a patient's symptoms without explaining every aspect of the clinical reasoning behind the subsequent decision.

The practitioner investigating the matter should therefore distinguish between:

  • what the patient reported;
  • what the practitioner observed;
  • what investigations were available;
  • what assessment was recorded;
  • what decision was made; and
  • what action followed.

These elements should then be placed into the wider chronology.

3. A Fictional Practitioner Note

The following is a fictional reconstruction created for training purposes. It does not represent a real patient or practitioner.

FICTIONAL PRACTITIONER PROGRESS NOTE Patient: Patient A
Date: 14 March
Time: 12:15
Clinical Assessment
Complaint
Increasing abdominal pain with ongoing nausea.
Observations
Temperature 38.4 °C. Pulse 116. Blood pressure 102/64.
Investigation
Blood tests pending.
Assessment
Ongoing abdominal symptoms requiring continued assessment.
Plan
Monitoring
Continue clinical observations.
Treatment
Continue supportive treatment.
Review
Review when investigation results are available or earlier if condition changes.
Notice the structure.

The note contains symptoms, observations, investigations, an assessment and a plan.

The practitioner must determine how each part of the note fits into the overall clinical chronology.

4. Clinical Decisions

A medical-negligence investigation will often focus on a particular clinical decision.

Examples may include a decision to:

  • investigate or not investigate;
  • admit or discharge a patient;
  • prescribe or withhold treatment;
  • refer the patient to another practitioner;
  • perform a procedure;
  • continue monitoring; or
  • seek further specialist assistance.

The record should therefore be examined for the circumstances surrounding the decision rather than only the eventual outcome.

5. Reconstructing the Decision Point

When a particular decision becomes important to the case, reconstruct the information available at that moment.

Question What to Identify
Patient What symptoms, complaints and changes in condition were present?
Observations What objective clinical information was available?
Investigations What results were available at that point?
History What relevant history had been obtained?
Assessment What clinical assessment was recorded?
Decision What course of action was selected?
Response What happened after the decision?
A decision should be analysed in the context of the information available when the decision was made.

6. What Was Known at the Time?

This question is central to the investigation of a clinical decision.

Later events may make an earlier diagnosis or clinical problem appear obvious.

The practitioner must avoid automatically transferring that later knowledge back into the earlier decision-making process.

Decision-point analysis

Identify the information available immediately before the decision was made.

Then identify what decision was made and what happened afterwards.

7. Documentation of Reasoning

Some practitioner notes contain detailed reasoning.

Others may record the conclusion or plan without setting out every part of the reasoning process.

The practitioner investigating the claim should accurately distinguish between what the document expressly states and what must be inferred from the surrounding evidence.

Ask:

Does the note expressly record the reasoning, or am I reconstructing the reasoning from the other evidence?

8. Interrogating Practitioner Notes

A systematic review should ask what the note tells us about the clinical decision-making process.

Question 1 — What information was available?

Identify the symptoms, history, observations and investigation results known at the relevant time.

Question 2 — What assessment was made?

Identify the diagnosis, differential diagnosis, clinical impression or other assessment recorded.

Question 3 — What decision was made?

Identify the treatment, investigation, referral, monitoring or other action selected.

Question 4 — Why?

Determine whether the record expressly documents the reason for the decision.

Question 5 — What happened afterwards?

Compare the decision with the subsequent clinical events.

Question 6 — Was the decision revisited?

Determine whether the practitioner reassessed the patient when new information or deterioration emerged.

9. Comparing Practitioner Notes With Other Records

Practitioner notes should be compared with the other parts of the medical record.

This may reveal consistency, discrepancies or missing information.

For example:

A practitioner note may record that the patient was stable, while the observation chart may show a sequence of changing vital signs.

That difference does not automatically establish negligence. It identifies a question that requires further investigation.

The practitioner should determine whether the apparent difference is explained by timing, terminology, clinical context or another part of the record.

10. The Practitioner Test

Before moving to the next category of medical record, ask:

Can I identify what information was available to the practitioner, what assessment was made, what decision was taken, and what happened afterwards?

If not, the practitioner notes must be read alongside the surrounding clinical records before the decision can be properly reconstructed.

Legal and Evidentiary Note

Practitioner notes form part of the documentary evidence from which the clinical decision-making process may be reconstructed.

The record should be assessed in the context of the complete evidentiary picture. A clinical note may record a conclusion without documenting every aspect of the reasoning that preceded it.

The practitioner should therefore distinguish between the contents of the document, reasonable inferences and matters that require additional evidence or expert interpretation.
