Chapter 2 — The Admission Record

Reading the first clinical document as evidence of the patient's entry into care

1. What Is the Admission Record?

The admission record is often one of the first significant documents created when a patient enters a hospital or healthcare facility.

It may record information about why the patient presented, the patient's condition on arrival, relevant history, initial observations, preliminary assessments and the initial plan of care.

For the medical-negligence practitioner, the admission record can therefore provide an important starting point for reconstructing the clinical circumstances in which subsequent decisions were made.

Practitioner's Principle

The admission record establishes the clinical starting point. Before judging what happened later, establish what was known when the patient first entered care.

2. Why the Admission Record Matters

A negligence investigation often focuses heavily on what happened later in the patient's treatment.

That can create a risk of hindsight.

The admission record helps the practitioner return to the information available at the beginning of the clinical episode.

It may help establish:

  • the patient's presenting complaint;
  • the symptoms reported at admission;
  • relevant medical history;
  • initial observations;
  • the urgency or apparent severity of the presentation;
  • the initial clinical assessment; and
  • the initial treatment or investigation plan.

3. A Fictional Admission Record

The following is a fictional reconstruction created for training purposes. It does not represent a real patient or healthcare facility.

Do not read this record only for what it says.

Ask what the recorded information means for the clinical situation at the time.

The important question is not simply whether the patient was documented as having abdominal pain. The practitioner must consider what information was available and what decisions followed from that information.

4. Establishing the Patient's Starting Condition

The admission record can help establish the patient's condition at the beginning of the relevant clinical episode.

This is important because later deterioration must be considered against the patient's initial presentation.

A patient who arrives already seriously ill presents a different evidentiary question from a patient who initially appears stable and subsequently deteriorates.

Litigation Question

What was the patient's condition at the point when the healthcare provider first assumed responsibility for care?

The answer should be supported by the contemporaneous record wherever possible.

5. The Importance of Timing

Timing is often one of the most important features of an admission record.

The practitioner should identify when the patient arrived, when observations were taken, when the patient was assessed, when investigations were requested and when treatment began.

These times can later be compared with other documents.

  • nursing notes;
  • practitioner progress notes;
  • laboratory results;
  • medication records;
  • observation charts; and
  • procedure or theatre records.
A chronology is built by connecting documents, not by reading each document in isolation.

6. Interrogating the Admission Record

Once the admission record has been identified, the practitioner should move from reading to investigation.

Question 1 — Presentation

What exactly did the patient present with?

Question 2 — Duration

How long had the symptoms or problem existed before the patient entered the healthcare system?

Question 3 — Initial Condition

What do the recorded observations tell us about the patient's condition at the relevant time?

Question 4 — Information Available

What information was available to the healthcare provider when the initial assessment was made?

Question 5 — Initial Response

What investigations, treatment or monitoring were initiated?

Question 6 — Missing Information

What information would ordinarily be expected but is not recorded in the document?

7. The Admission Record and Hindsight

Medical-negligence litigation is often examined after the patient's outcome is known.

That creates a danger of judging an earlier clinical decision using information that became available only later.

The admission record can help prevent that error by identifying the information available at the beginning of the episode.

Practitioner's Principle

Ask what the practitioner knew, or reasonably ought to have known, at the relevant time — not merely what became obvious after the patient's condition changed.

8. Comparing the Admission Record With the Patient's Account

The admission record should be compared with the patient's account obtained during the initial consultation.

The two accounts may correspond closely, or they may differ.

A difference does not automatically establish that either account is false.

Instead, the discrepancy should become an investigation point.

For example:

If the patient recalls reporting severe symptoms on arrival, but the admission record describes only mild symptoms, determine what other evidence may assist in resolving the difference.

Other nursing records, triage documents, observations, witness evidence and subsequent clinical notes may assist.

9. Building the First Chronology

The admission record should be incorporated into the developing medical chronology.

Record the following:
  • Date and time of admission
  • Presenting complaint
  • Duration of symptoms
  • Relevant history
  • Initial observations
  • Initial assessment
  • Investigations requested
  • Initial treatment
  • Planned review or monitoring

10. The Practitioner Test

Before moving to the next category of medical record, ask:

Can I describe the patient's condition and the information available at the beginning of the clinical episode without relying on hindsight?

If not, return to the admission record and identify what additional documents are required.

Legal and Evidentiary Note

The admission record is documentary evidence. Its significance depends on the circumstances of the case and must be considered together with the other evidence.

The practitioner should distinguish between what is expressly recorded, what may reasonably be inferred, and what remains uncertain.

The record should therefore be used to build the factual foundation of the investigation rather than treated as an automatic answer to the legal question of negligence.
