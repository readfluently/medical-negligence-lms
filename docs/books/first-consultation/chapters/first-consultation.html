The First Consultation

The first consultation is not simply an opportunity to hear the client's story. It is the beginning of an investigation.

Practitioner's Principle
Investigate the story. Do not decide the case too early.

The Case Map

The investigation should begin by trying to build a coherent map:

  • Patient
  • Pre-treatment condition
  • Medical event
  • Treatment
  • Alleged problem
  • Injury
  • Subsequent treatment
  • Current condition
  • Loss

Around this central sequence sit the records, witnesses, healthcare providers, potential defendants, potential experts, dates and unanswered questions.

Why Dates Matter

In a medical-negligence case, time may become relevant to:

  • the medical chronology
  • the sequence of clinical decisions
  • the development of the injury
  • causation
  • prescription
  • statutory notice
  • procedural steps
  • expert opinions

The Medical Chronology

Why it matters

The chronology is the factual spine of the investigation. It should identify what happened, when it happened, who was involved, what information was available at each stage and what happened afterwards.

Practitioner questions

  • When did the relevant symptoms begin?
  • When did the patient first seek treatment?
  • What investigations were performed?
  • What treatment was recommended?
  • When did the alleged complication become apparent?
  • What subsequent treatment occurred?

Evidence to obtain

  • Clinical records
  • Referral records
  • Investigation results
  • Medication records
  • Operative or procedure notes
  • Subsequent treatment records
A chronology should identify uncertainty as well as certainty. Missing information is itself an investigative finding.

The Sequence of Clinical Decisions

The investigative question

The practitioner should reconstruct the sequence of clinical decisions rather than simply recording the eventual outcome.

The critical question is what was known or reasonably observable at the relevant point in time and what decision followed from it.

Questions for the file

  • What information was available to the healthcare provider?
  • What signs or symptoms were recorded?
  • What investigation was ordered or omitted?
  • What treatment decision followed?
  • Was the patient monitored appropriately?
  • What happened immediately afterwards?
Do not assess a clinical decision only with hindsight. Reconstruct the information available when the decision was made.

The Development of the Injury

What must be reconstructed?

The investigation must distinguish the patient's condition before the relevant medical event from the condition afterwards.

The practitioner should identify the earliest evidence of the alleged injury and trace its development through subsequent treatment and investigation.

Questions

  • What was the patient's condition before treatment?
  • When was the alleged injury first documented?
  • Was the injury immediate or progressive?
  • What alternative explanations exist?
  • What subsequent treatment affected the condition?
The existence of harm does not, by itself, establish who or what caused it.

Causation

Why causation requires separate investigation

Establishing an alleged departure from proper medical care is not the end of the enquiry. The practitioner must investigate whether the alleged wrongful and negligent conduct caused the loss complained of.

In AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape, the Supreme Court of Appeal dealt directly with factual causation in a medical negligence claim involving failure to monitor a mother and foetus during labour. The court distinguished wrongfulness from factual causation and considered whether the alleged negligent conduct had caused the injury.

SCA AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape

[2019] ZASCA 102

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Damage-causing event

In MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q, the SCA considered the plaintiff's burden to prove the damage-causing occurrence and cautioned against reasoning backwards from the existence of injury to negligence.

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

[2018] ZASCA 132

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner questions

  • What is the alleged negligent act or omission?
  • What injury is said to have resulted?
  • What alternative causes must be investigated?
  • What medical evidence connects the event to the injury?
  • Is the causal chain supported by contemporaneous records?
Wrongfulness, negligence and causation are related questions, but they should not be collapsed into one enquiry.

Prescription

Why it belongs in the first investigation

Prescription should not be left until the end of the file. Dates, knowledge and the identity of the relevant debt can become important from the beginning of the investigation.

Primary authority

Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province is an important Constitutional Court authority concerning prescription in a medical-negligence context.

Constitutional Court Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province

2016 (4) SA 414 (CC)

The case is concerned with the factual knowledge relevant to when prescription begins to run.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Subsequent authority

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v M C

[2020] ZASCA 165

The SCA considered the relationship between knowledge of primary facts and the commencement of prescription in a medical-negligence claim.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner questions

  • When did the relevant debt become due?
  • What material facts were known?
  • When were those facts known?
  • When could they reasonably have been known?
  • Was there interruption or delay recognised by law?
  • Is the claim against an organ of state?
Prescription analysis should be based on the applicable statutory framework and the established facts, not simply the date on which the client says they discovered negligence.

Statutory Notice

When it becomes relevant

Claims involving organs of state require early attention to statutory notice requirements.

The Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 regulates notice requirements for certain proceedings against organs of state.

Legislation Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002

Consult the current consolidated text when assessing a live matter.

Read the Act on SAFLII →

Notice investigation

  • Identify the correct defendant.
  • Determine whether it falls within the statutory framework.
  • Identify when the debt became due.
  • Check whether notice was given.
  • Check when and how notice was served.
  • Preserve proof of service.
  • Investigate any possible condonation issue immediately.

Recent authority

High Court Badenhorst and Others v MEC for Health, North West Province

[2026] ZANWHC 168

The judgment considers statutory notice and prescription issues and refers to authorities including Links, Loni and Madinda.

Procedural Steps

Build the procedural map early

The practitioner should identify the procedural requirements alongside the factual and medical investigation.

  • Obtain complete records.
  • Construct the chronology.
  • Identify healthcare providers.
  • Identify potential defendants.
  • Identify outstanding records.
  • Identify witnesses.
  • Identify expert questions.
  • Investigate prescription.
  • Investigate statutory notice.
  • Preserve relevant evidence.
Procedure should be mapped alongside the investigation, not after the investigation has supposedly finished.

Expert Opinions

Start with the question

Expert selection should begin with the specialised question that must be answered, rather than simply selecting a medical specialty.

Examples of questions

  • What was the appropriate clinical response?
  • Was the relevant investigation indicated?
  • Was the treatment appropriate?
  • Was there a departure from the applicable standard?
  • What caused the injury?
  • Could another mechanism explain the injury?

Important authority

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

[2018] ZASCA 132

The SCA considered the logical basis and probative value of expert opinion in a medical-negligence dispute.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →
Do not begin by asking, “Which expert do we need?” Begin by asking, “What specialised question must be answered?”

Closing Principle
The first consultation should produce a structured investigation, not a premature conclusion.
