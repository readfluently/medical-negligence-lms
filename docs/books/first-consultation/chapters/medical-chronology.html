The Medical Chronology

A medical-negligence investigation should begin by converting the client's story into a reliable sequence of events.

Practitioner's Principle
A chronology is not merely a list of dates. It is a method of testing the factual story against the available evidence.

What Are You Trying to Build?

The chronology should allow the practitioner to see the patient's journey from the condition that existed before the relevant medical event through treatment, the alleged problem, injury, subsequent treatment and the patient's current condition.

The chronology should therefore connect:

  • the patient;
  • the pre-treatment condition;
  • the medical event;
  • the treatment;
  • the alleged problem;
  • the injury;
  • subsequent treatment;
  • the current condition; and
  • the alleged loss.

Dates Matter

Dates may become relevant to several different aspects of the investigation, including:

  • the sequence of clinical decisions
  • the development of the injury
  • causation
  • prescription
  • statutory notice
  • procedural steps
  • expert opinions

The Sequence of Clinical Decisions

Build the sequence

The practitioner should identify the clinical events in the order in which they occurred.

This means recording the relevant consultation, observation, investigation, result, decision, treatment and subsequent response.

Questions to ask

  • What happened first?
  • What information was available at that point?
  • What decision was made?
  • What happened immediately afterwards?
  • When did the next significant event occur?
The sequence should be reconstructed from the records and other evidence, not solely from memory.

The Development of the Injury

Do not start with the final condition

A useful chronology starts before the alleged negligent event. The patient's condition before treatment may become important when assessing what changed afterwards.

Capture the progression

  • Pre-existing symptoms or condition
  • Initial consultation
  • Relevant treatment
  • First indication of deterioration
  • Diagnosis or investigation of the problem
  • Subsequent treatment
  • Current condition
The chronology should show how the patient's condition changed, rather than simply recording that an injury eventually existed.

Causation

Use the chronology to test the causal story

The chronology should allow the practitioner to ask whether the timing of events supports the alleged causal connection between the medical event and the injury.

This does not mean that chronology alone proves causation. Rather, chronology identifies the factual sequence that must subsequently be tested through the appropriate evidence.

Questions for the investigation

  • When did the alleged problem first appear?
  • What happened immediately before it?
  • What happened immediately afterwards?
  • Were there other possible causes?
  • What do the subsequent medical records show?
Chronology is an investigative tool for causation; it is not a substitute for the evidence required to establish causation.

Prescription: When Does the Clock Start?

1. The basic rule

Prescription is one of the first issues that should be investigated in a medical-negligence matter. It should not be left until the factual and expert investigation is complete.

Section 11(d) of the Prescription Act 68 of 1969 generally provides a period of three years for "any other debt", unless another law provides otherwise.

Legislation Prescription Act 68 of 1969 — Section 11(d)

Section 11(d) provides a three-year period for any other debt, subject to the other provisions of the Act and legislation providing otherwise.

Read the Prescription Act — Department of Justice →

2. Medical negligence is not simply "three years from the operation"

A common mistake is to calculate three years automatically from the date of the operation, consultation, injection, procedure or other medical event.

That is not the statutory test. Section 12(1) provides that, subject to the Act, prescription generally commences when the debt becomes due.

Section 12(3) is particularly important for a non-contractual debt. A debt is not deemed to be due until the creditor has knowledge of:

  • the identity of the debtor; and
  • the facts from which the debt arises.

The section contains an important qualification: the creditor is deemed to have such knowledge if it could have acquired it by exercising reasonable care.

3. The leading medical-negligence authority

SCA Truter and Another v Deysel

[2006] ZASCA 16; 2006 (4) SA 168 (SCA)

The case specifically concerned the commencement of prescription in a claim for damages arising from alleged medical negligence. The SCA considered what is meant by "knowledge of the facts from which the debt arises" under section 12(3).

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

4. The knowledge enquiry

The practitioner should therefore avoid asking only:

The wrong question
"When did the operation take place?"

The better questions are:

The prescription questions
When did the claimant acquire knowledge of the identity of the debtor, and when did the claimant acquire knowledge of the material facts from which the debt arose?

5. What are the "facts from which the debt arises"?

This is often the difficult part of a medical-negligence prescription enquiry.

The practitioner must first identify the primary facts relevant to the debt and then determine when those facts were actually known or could reasonably have been known.

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v M C

[2020] ZASCA 165

The SCA described section 12(3) as involving two enquiries: identifying the primary facts from which the debt arises and determining when those facts were known or should reasonably have been known.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

6. Does the patient have to know that the doctor was negligent?

No, not in the sense of knowing the legal conclusion.

The authorities distinguish between knowledge of the material facts and knowledge of the legal conclusion that those facts constitute negligence.

The claimant does not necessarily have to know the legal rule, the legal characterisation of the conduct, or the ultimate conclusion that the healthcare provider was negligent.

SCA Truter and Another v Deysel

[2006] ZASCA 16; 2006 (4) SA 168 (SCA)

The SCA's reasoning distinguishes factual knowledge from knowledge of the legal conclusion that the facts constitute negligence.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

7. Links: medical-negligence knowledge can be factually difficult

Constitutional Court Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province

[2016] ZACC 10; 2016 (4) SA 414 (CC)

This is a leading Constitutional Court authority concerning the knowledge enquiry in a medical-negligence context.

The case is particularly important where a patient knows that an adverse medical outcome occurred but the factual basis for attributing that outcome to fault is disputed.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

8. Actual knowledge and deemed knowledge

Section 12(3) creates an important distinction between:

  • Actual knowledge: the claimant in fact knew the relevant information.
  • Knowledge obtainable through reasonable care: the claimant is deemed to have knowledge where the relevant information could have been acquired by exercising reasonable care.

The practitioner should investigate both what the claimant actually knew and what the evidence establishes about what the claimant reasonably could have known.

9. Does prescription wait for an expert opinion?

The practitioner should not assume that prescription can only begin after an expert has issued a report saying that negligence occurred.

The prescription enquiry concerns the claimant's knowledge of the relevant facts. The question is therefore not simply whether an expert had yet expressed an opinion.

SCA WK Construction (Pty) Ltd v Moores Rowland and Others

[2022] ZASCA 44; 2022 (6) SA 180 (SCA)

Although not itself a medical-negligence case, the SCA's discussion of professional negligence is useful when analysing section 12(3). The court distinguished the facts needed to trigger prescription from the full evidence that may ultimately be required to prove negligence at trial.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

10. Identifying the debtor is a separate enquiry

Section 12(3) requires attention to the identity of the debtor as well as the facts from which the debt arises.

This becomes particularly important where several healthcare providers, hospitals, practitioners or other entities may have played different roles in treatment.

Constitutional Court Mtokonya v Minister of Police

[2017] ZACC 33; 2018 (5) SA 22 (CC)

The Constitutional Court considered the identity-of-debtor requirement and the distinction between knowledge of facts and knowledge of the legal consequences of those facts.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

11. Who bears the burden?

The party relying on prescription bears the onus of establishing the prescription defence.

In practical terms, a prescription defence should identify the relevant facts, establish when the claimant had the legally relevant knowledge, and then demonstrate when the applicable period expired.

High Court — 2026 Selepe v Member of the Executive Council for Health, North West Province

[2026] ZANWHC 262

The judgment, handed down on 30 September 2026, concerned a medical-negligence prescription special plea and reiterates the importance of the party relying on prescription invoking and proving the defence.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

12. Prescription versus statutory notice

A medical-negligence claim against an organ of state may involve another statutory timing regime in addition to prescription.

The Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 generally requires notice of intended proceedings to be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due.

This six-month notice requirement must not be confused with the ordinary three-year prescription period under the Prescription Act.

Legislation Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 — Section 3

The Act regulates notice requirements for proceedings against certain organs of state. The South African Government source identifies the Act as regulating prescription and notice requirements.

Read the Act on gov.za →

Read the amended consolidated Act on SAFLII →

13. The six-month notice period does not replace prescription

The practitioner should therefore maintain two separate timing tracks where the defendant is an organ of state.

Track A — Prescription
Determine when the debt became due under the Prescription Act, then calculate the applicable prescription period.
Track B — Statutory Notice
Determine when the debt became due and assess whether the statutory notice requirement was met within the applicable period.

14. The 60-day procedural rule after notice

Section 5(2) of the Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 creates an additional procedural timing rule.

Generally, process instituting proceedings may not be served before the expiry of 60 days after the notice has been served, unless the organ of state has repudiated the creditor's claim in writing before the expiry of that period.

Legislation State Organs Act — Section 5(2)
Read the Act on SAFLII →

15. Interruption and other prescription issues

The practitioner should not stop the analysis after identifying a preliminary prescription date.

The Prescription Act contains further provisions concerning matters that may affect the completion or interruption of prescription.

  • Section 13 — certain delays in completion of prescription
  • Section 14 — interruption by acknowledgement of liability
  • Section 15 — interruption through service of process

These provisions should be investigated against the actual history of the matter.

Legislation Prescription Act 68 of 1969
Consult the Prescription Act →

16. Prescription of the Medical-Negligence Claim versus Prescription of the Judgment Debt

A critical distinction must be made between the prescription of the original medical-negligence claim and the prescription of a debt that has subsequently become a judgment debt.

The key distinction
Before judgment, the practitioner is ordinarily dealing with the prescription of the underlying medical-negligence or delictual debt.

After judgment, the resulting obligation falls within the judgment-debt prescription regime provided by section 11(a)(ii) of the Prescription Act.

The ordinary medical-negligence debt

A medical-negligence claim will ordinarily fall within section 11(d), which provides a three-year period for "any other debt", subject to the Act and any applicable legislation.

The practitioner must first determine when the underlying debt became due under section 12 before calculating the applicable period.

What happens when judgment is granted?

Section 11(a)(ii) of the Prescription Act provides a thirty-year prescription period in respect of any judgment debt.

This is not a 30-year period in which to institute the original medical-negligence claim. It is the prescription period applicable to a judgment debt.

Legislation Prescription Act — Section 11(a)(ii)

Section 11(a)(ii) provides a 30-year period for any judgment debt.

Read the Prescription Act — Department of Justice →

It does NOT mean that every medical-negligence claim has 30 years

Before judgment

The underlying medical-negligence debt is ordinarily subject to the applicable prescription period under the Prescription Act.

After judgment

The resulting judgment debt falls within the judgment-debt prescription category in section 11(a)(ii).

Section 15(4): prescription commences afresh after judgment

Section 15 deals with interruption of prescription by service of process. Where prescription is interrupted by service of process and judgment is subsequently given, section 15(4) provides that prescription commences to run afresh on the day on which the judgment becomes executable.

SCA Eley (formerly Memmel) v Lynn & Main Inc

[2007] ZASCA 142; 2008 (2) SA 151 (SCA)

The SCA considered sections 11(a)(ii) and 15(4), including the statutory distinction between the three-year category in section 11(d) and the 30-year period applicable to a judgment debt.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

The timeline

Stage 1 — Medical event
Treatment or another relevant medical event occurs.

↓

Stage 2 — Debt becomes due
Apply section 12 and, where relevant, section 12(3) to determine when the underlying debt became due.

↓

Stage 3 — Ordinary prescription period
The applicable prescription period runs against the underlying debt.

↓

Stage 4 — Institution of proceedings
Service of process may interrupt prescription in accordance with section 15.

↓

Stage 5 — Judgment
If judgment is given and becomes executable, section 15(4) provides for prescription to commence afresh.

↓

Stage 6 — Judgment debt
The judgment debt is subject to the 30-year period provided by section 11(a)(ii).

A common misconception

Incorrect
"Medical-negligence claims prescribe after 30 years."
Incorrect
"The three-year period only starts once the court has determined that the doctor was negligent."

The correct analysis is to distinguish the underlying claim from the judgment debt that may arise after successful institution of proceedings.

Why this matters in a practitioner file

Practitioner takeaway
Do not confuse the prescription of the original medical-negligence debt with the prescription of a judgment debt. The two stages are governed by different provisions of the Prescription Act.

17. A Worked Example

Consider the following hypothetical sequence:

1 January 2022
Patient undergoes surgery.

January 2022
Patient experiences complications.

2022–2023
Further treatment takes place.

15 August 2023
Patient receives information giving rise to a reasonable basis for investigating whether the injury may have resulted from fault in treatment.

20 August 2023
Identity of the relevant healthcare provider is established.

It would be unsafe simply to calculate three years from 1 January 2022 merely because that was the date of the operation.

The practitioner must determine, on the actual evidence and applicable law, when the debt became due and what knowledge existed for purposes of section 12(3).

18. Recent Illustration: M.R.M v Lenmed Health Group Limited

High Court — 2026 M.R.M v Lenmed Health Group Limited and Others

[2026] ZAGPJHC 416

The judgment concerned a medical-negligence claim in which a doctor raised a prescription defence. On the facts before the court, the claimant's relevant knowledge included knowledge of the debtor and the material facts at a particular point in the litigation history, leading to a finding that the claim against that doctor had prescribed.

19. Recent Illustration: Badenhorst

High Court — 2026 Badenhorst and Others v MEC for Health, North West Province

[2026] ZANWHC 168

This medical-negligence matter concerned both statutory notice and prescription. The applicants sought condonation for the late notice. The court granted condonation on the facts and concluded that the debt had not been extinguished by prescription.

The case should not be read as establishing a general rule that an HPCSA investigation postpones prescription. Its significance is in the factual application of the statutory requirements.

21. Practitioner Deadline Checklist

The Core Practitioner Rule
Do not write "three years from the operation" into the file without first identifying when the debt became due under section 12 and what the claimant knew, or is deemed to have known, under section 12(3).
Research hierarchy: Start with the applicable legislation, then consult Constitutional Court authority, Supreme Court of Appeal authority, and subsequent High Court decisions applying those principles.

Currentness: This chapter includes selected authorities available as at October 2026. Case law and legislation should always be checked against the current authoritative text before reliance in a live matter.

Important: Prescription is highly fact-specific. This chapter is a practitioner research aid and is not a substitute for analysing the pleadings, evidence, legislation and current case law in an individual matter.

Statutory Notice

Build notice dates into the chronology

Where the proposed defendant is an organ of state, the chronology should separately record events relevant to any applicable statutory notice requirement.

Record

  • Date on which the relevant debt may have become due
  • Date on which notice was prepared
  • Date on which notice was served
  • Method of service
  • Recipient
  • Proof of service
  • Any subsequent correspondence

Procedural Steps

Chronology should include procedural events

The chronology should not stop at the patient's treatment. Important legal and investigative events should also be recorded.

  • Request for medical records
  • Receipt of medical records
  • Outstanding record requests
  • Consultation with witnesses
  • Expert referral
  • Expert opinion received
  • Statutory notice
  • Relevant correspondence
  • Institution of proceedings
A litigation chronology should allow another practitioner to understand both the medical history and the development of the file.

Expert Opinions

Experts need a reliable factual sequence

An expert should be given sufficient factual material to understand the relevant sequence of events.

The chronology can therefore become an important working document when identifying the specialised questions that require an expert opinion.

Before sending a file to an expert

  • Check the chronology against the clinical records.
  • Identify missing records.
  • Identify disputed dates.
  • Identify assumptions.
  • Identify the precise expert question.
The better the factual sequence, the clearer the specialised question presented to the expert.

Building the Chronology

A useful working chronology can contain at least the following fields:

Date
When did the event occur?

Event
What happened?

Healthcare provider
Who was involved?

Evidence
Which document, witness or other source supports the entry?

Clinical significance
Why might the event matter?

Legal significance
Could the date affect causation, prescription, notice or procedure?

Outstanding question
What remains uncertain?

The Practitioner's Test

Before moving from chronology into legal conclusions, ask:

  • What do we know?
  • How do we know it?
  • What is missing?
  • Which dates are disputed?
  • Which dates require documentary proof?
  • Which dates may have legal significance?
  • What question must an expert answer?
Closing Principle
The chronology should expose the gaps in the story as clearly as it records the events that are already established.
