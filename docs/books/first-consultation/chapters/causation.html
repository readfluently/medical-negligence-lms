Causation

Causation is the bridge between the conduct complained of and the harm for which compensation is claimed.

A medical-negligence investigation may establish that a healthcare provider acted wrongfully or negligently. That does not, by itself, establish that the conduct caused the patient's injury.

Practitioner's Principle
Do not ask only: "Was there negligence?"

Ask also: "Did the negligence cause the harm complained of?"

The Two Causation Questions

South African delictual law distinguishes between factual causation and legal causation.

Factual causation
Did the wrongful conduct cause or materially contribute to the harm?

Legal causation
Is the connection between the conduct and the harm sufficiently close for liability to be imposed?
SCA Minister of Safety and Security v Van Duivenboden

[2002] ZASCA 79; 2002 (6) SA 431 (SCA)

The SCA explained that causation involves two distinct enquiries: whether the wrongful conduct was a factual cause of the loss and whether it is sufficiently closely connected to the loss for legal responsibility to follow.

Factual Causation

The central question

Factual causation asks whether the defendant's wrongful conduct actually caused or materially contributed to the harm suffered.

In a straightforward case, the enquiry asks what would probably have happened if the relevant wrongful conduct had not occurred.

SCA — Medical Negligence AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape

[2019] ZASCA 102

The SCA held that even where hospital staff had acted wrongfully and negligently, the plaintiff still had to prove that the negligent failure caused the child's brain injury. Wrongfulness and factual causation were separate enquiries.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner questions

  • What precisely was the wrongful or negligent conduct?
  • What injury is alleged?
  • What causal mechanism is proposed?
  • What would probably have happened without the conduct?
  • What evidence supports that conclusion?

The But-For Test

The counterfactual enquiry

The traditional factual-causation enquiry asks:

If the defendant had acted lawfully and properly, would the harm probably still have occurred?

Where the alleged negligence is an omission, the practitioner may need to construct a hypothetical course of lawful conduct and compare it with what actually occurred.

Constitutional Court Mashongwa v PRASA

[2015] ZACC 36; 2016 (3) SA 528 (CC)

The Constitutional Court confirmed the continuing importance of the traditional but-for test and explained that where it is adequate to establish factual causation, a different approach need not be used.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Medical-negligence example

Suppose the allegation is that a practitioner failed to recognise a patient's deterioration.

The question is not simply:

"The patient deteriorated after the practitioner saw the patient."

The enquiry is instead whether, if the practitioner had properly recognised and acted upon the relevant information, the injury would probably have been avoided or reduced.

Causation by Omission

Why omissions are difficult

Medical-negligence claims frequently concern an alleged failure to do something:

  • failure to diagnose;
  • failure to investigate;
  • failure to monitor;
  • failure to refer;
  • failure to treat;
  • failure to intervene;
  • failure to respond to deterioration.

The causation enquiry requires the practitioner to identify the lawful course of conduct that should be inserted into the counterfactual.

SCA — Medical Negligence AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape

[2019] ZASCA 102

The court asked whether proper monitoring and appropriate intervention would probably have prevented the brain injury. Because that proposition was not proved on the evidence, factual causation failed.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Material Contribution

The medical-negligence enquiry may sometimes be expressed by asking whether the defendant's conduct caused or materially contributed to the harm.

This language should not be used as a substitute for analysing the actual causal question.

SCA — Medical Negligence AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape

[2019] ZASCA 102

The SCA stated the factual-causation test in terms of whether the defendant's act or omission caused or materially contributed to the harm.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →
Always identify the precise mechanism by which the alleged conduct is said to have contributed to the injury.

Legal Causation

Even factual causation is not the end

Establishing factual causation does not automatically answer whether legal liability should follow.

The second enquiry considers whether the connection between the wrongful conduct and the loss is sufficiently close for liability to be imposed.

Constitutional Court Mashongwa v PRASA

[2015] ZACC 36

The Constitutional Court described legal causation as concerned with the closeness of the connection between harmful conduct and harm and the policy considerations relevant to imputing liability.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner questions

  • Is the harm sufficiently closely connected to the conduct?
  • Is the harm too remote?
  • Did an intervening event materially alter the causal chain?
  • Do considerations of fairness and justice support liability?

Balance of Probabilities

Causation in a civil medical-negligence claim is ordinarily determined on the balance of probabilities.

The task is not to prove causation with absolute scientific certainty.

SCA Minister of Safety and Security v Van Duivenboden

[2002] ZASCA 79

The SCA described the factual-causation enquiry as a retrospective assessment of what probably would have occurred, based on the evidence and ordinary human experience.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →
The question is not whether another outcome was scientifically impossible. The question is what the evidence establishes as probable.

Alternative Causes

The practitioner must investigate whether another mechanism could explain the patient's injury.

Possible alternatives may include:

  • pre-existing disease;
  • natural progression;
  • an unrelated medical event;
  • complication of treatment;
  • subsequent treatment;
  • another healthcare provider's conduct;
  • trauma or another external event.
An alternative cause does not automatically defeat the claim. It must be evaluated against the totality of the evidence.
SCA — Medical Negligence MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

[2018] ZASCA 132

The SCA emphasised that the plaintiff bears the onus of establishing the damage-causing event and that negligence cannot simply be inferred by reasoning backwards from the existence of injury.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Expert Evidence on Causation

Identify the specialised question

Medical causation often requires specialised evidence concerning the mechanism, timing, probability and consequences of an injury.

The practitioner should therefore formulate the question before selecting the expert.

Examples:

What mechanism probably caused the injury?

Is the injury consistent with the alleged medical event?

Would proper treatment probably have prevented or reduced the injury?

Was the injury already developing before the alleged breach?

Did a subsequent event materially affect the patient's condition?
SCA Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic

[2001] ZASCA 12; 2001 (3) SA 1188 (SCA)

The SCA explained that expert opinions should be evaluated according to their logical basis and reasoning. The court remains responsible for determining the issue.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →
Do not ask the expert to decide the legal case. Ask the expert to answer the medical question requiring specialised knowledge.

Reverse Reasoning

Reverse reasoning occurs when the investigator begins with the existence of an injury and reasons backwards to the conclusion that the healthcare provider must have been negligent.

Avoid this reasoning
The patient was injured.
↓
Therefore the treatment must have been negligent.
↓
Therefore the negligence caused the injury.

Each proposition requires its own investigation.

SCA — Medical Negligence MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

[2018] ZASCA 132

The SCA expressly rejected impermissible reverse reasoning. An inference of negligence requires evidence establishing the relevant damage-causing event and the appropriate causal connection.

Worked Example

A patient presents to a hospital with symptoms. The practitioner fails to identify a significant deterioration, the patient is discharged, and the patient later suffers serious harm.

The premature conclusion would be:

"The practitioner failed to diagnose the condition, therefore the failure caused the injury."

A proper investigation asks:

1. What signs and symptoms were present?

2. What information was available to the practitioner?

3. What should reasonably have been recognised?

4. What would proper assessment probably have led to?

5. What treatment would probably have followed?

6. Would that treatment probably have prevented or reduced the injury?

7. What alternative causes exist?

8. What medical evidence addresses those questions?

The Practitioner's Test

Before concluding that causation has been established, ask:

  • Have I separated negligence from causation?
  • Have I identified the precise injury?
  • Have I established when the injury occurred?
  • Have I identified the alleged causal mechanism?
  • Have I applied the correct counterfactual?
  • Have I investigated alternative causes?
  • Is the expert reasoning logically supported?
  • Have I considered legal causation?
  • Am I reasoning from evidence, or merely from the eventual outcome?
Closing Principle
An injury tells you that harm occurred.

Negligence tells you that a standard may have been breached.

Causation asks whether that breach actually caused the harm for which liability is claimed.

Keep those questions separate.
