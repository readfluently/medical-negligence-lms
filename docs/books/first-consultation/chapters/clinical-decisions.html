The Sequence of Clinical Decisions

A medical-negligence investigation should not merely ask what eventually happened to the patient.

It should reconstruct the sequence of clinical decisions that preceded the outcome.

Practitioner's Principle
Reconstruct the decision before evaluating the decision.

Why the Sequence Matters

A patient's eventual injury may cause the earlier medical decisions to appear obvious in retrospect.

The practitioner must resist that temptation.

The investigation should establish:

  • what the patient presented with;
  • what the healthcare provider observed;
  • what information was available;
  • what investigations were performed or considered;
  • what findings were obtained;
  • what clinical decision was made;
  • what treatment followed;
  • what happened afterwards; and
  • whether the condition changed and required another decision.

Information Available at the Time

The question

The practitioner should establish what information was actually available to the healthcare provider when the clinical decision was made.

This may include:

  • the patient's symptoms;
  • the patient's history;
  • physical examination findings;
  • vital signs;
  • investigation results;
  • previous diagnoses;
  • medication history;
  • information communicated by other practitioners;
  • the patient's response to previous treatment.

Practitioner question

Ask:

What did the practitioner know, or have available, at the moment the decision was made?

This is different from asking what the practitioner eventually came to know after the patient's condition deteriorated.

Clinical Assessment

The investigation should identify how the patient's presentation was assessed at each important stage.

The practitioner should identify:

  • what symptoms were reported;
  • what findings were recorded;
  • what diagnosis was considered;
  • what differential diagnoses were considered where relevant;
  • what risk factors were identified;
  • what treatment was selected.
A diagnosis recorded in the file is a fact to investigate. It is not automatically proof that the diagnosis was correct or negligent.

Investigations

Do not ask only whether an investigation was performed

The practitioner should determine:

  • what investigation was considered;
  • why it was ordered;
  • when it was ordered;
  • when it was performed;
  • who performed it;
  • who interpreted the result;
  • when the result became available;
  • who received or reviewed the result;
  • what action followed.

The missing-investigation question

If an investigation was not performed, do not immediately record:

"The practitioner negligently failed to order the investigation."

Instead record the underlying factual proposition:

"The investigation was not recorded as having been ordered or performed."

The reason for the omission can then be investigated.

The Decision Point

A clinical decision should be identified as a distinct event in the chronology.

Examples include:

  • admit or discharge;
  • investigate or observe;
  • operate or continue conservative treatment;
  • refer or continue management;
  • change medication;
  • escalate treatment;
  • monitor or transfer.

For every important decision ask

  • What decision was made?
  • Who made it?
  • When was it made?
  • What information was available?
  • What alternatives were available?
  • What happened after the decision?

The Reasonable Practitioner

The legal enquiry

In professional negligence, the question is not whether the healthcare provider achieved a perfect outcome.

The investigation concerns the standard of conduct expected of a reasonably competent practitioner in the relevant circumstances.

Constitutional Court Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape

[2015] ZACC 33; 2016 (1) SA 325 (CC)

The Constitutional Court emphasised that reasonableness and negligence are matters for the court, assessed through the evidence, including competing expert opinions.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →
The question is not: "Could something better have been done?"

The question is: "What was reasonably expected of a practitioner in these circumstances?"

Clinical Guidelines

Guidelines may become important evidence when investigating whether a clinical decision departed from an accepted standard.

But a guideline should not automatically be treated as a complete answer to negligence.

SCA A M and Another v MEC for Health, Western Cape

[2020] ZASCA 89; 2021 (3) SA 337 (SCA)

The SCA considered a discharge decision and the relevance of professional standards and guidelines. It held that a departure from guidelines does not necessarily, by itself, establish negligence. The question remains whether the conduct departed from the standard of care reasonably expected in the circumstances.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner questions

  • Was there a guideline in force at the relevant time?
  • What did the guideline actually require?
  • Was it applicable to this clinical situation?
  • Was there evidence of compliance or departure?
  • Was there a clinical reason for departing from it?
  • Does the expert evidence explain the significance of the guideline?

Hindsight

The danger

Once the patient's eventual injury is known, earlier decisions may appear obviously wrong.

The investigation must distinguish knowledge available at the time from knowledge acquired only later.

Investigate before judging
Do not use the eventual outcome as a substitute for reconstructing the circumstances in which the decision was actually made.

This does not mean that hindsight is irrelevant to the case. Subsequent events may help establish what happened and may raise questions requiring expert investigation.

But the practitioner must still reconstruct the earlier decision on its own factual and clinical setting.

How to Reconstruct a Clinical Decision

For each significant decision, create a separate decision entry.

1. Presentation
What was the patient presenting with?

2. Information
What information was available?

3. Assessment
What was observed or assessed?

4. Investigation
What investigations were performed, ordered, considered or omitted?

5. Decision
What clinical decision was made?

6. Actor
Who made or implemented the decision?

7. Alternatives
What other options may have existed?

8. Response
What happened after the decision?

9. Escalation
Did the patient's condition require a further decision?

Decision Versus Outcome

A poor outcome does not, by itself, explain the quality of the decision that preceded it.

The practitioner should therefore create two separate columns mentally — and preferably in the working chronology:

The decision
What was decided at the time?

The outcome
What happened afterwards?

The two are connected, but they are not the same proposition.

The Records: Follow the Decision

The relevant records should be examined in the sequence in which the clinical decisions occurred.

  • triage records;
  • clinical notes;
  • nursing notes;
  • vital-sign records;
  • investigation requests;
  • laboratory results;
  • radiology;
  • medication charts;
  • referral records;
  • theatre records;
  • anaesthetic records;
  • discharge records;
  • subsequent treatment records.
Records question
What document should exist if this clinical decision actually occurred?

When the Records and the Client's Account Differ

The client's account and the medical records may not agree.

Do not immediately decide which version is correct.

Record the conflict.

Client's account
What does the patient say happened?

Contemporaneous record
What does the medical record say?

Witness evidence
Does another person provide direct evidence?

Expert interpretation
What specialised question remains?

Outstanding issue
What remains uncertain?
A disputed fact is not a reason to stop investigating. It is a reason to investigate more carefully.

Where Expert Evidence Enters

Experts may assist the practitioner and court in understanding specialised clinical questions.

But the expert should be asked a defined question based on the reconstructed factual sequence.

Constitutional Court Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape

[2015] ZACC 33

The Constitutional Court stressed that the court must evaluate expert evidence critically and determine reasonableness and negligence itself.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →
Do not ask the expert: "Was the doctor negligent?" First establish the specialised questions that allow the legal decision-maker to evaluate the conduct.

Worked Investigative Example

Consider a patient who attends a hospital with symptoms and is later discharged. The patient subsequently deteriorates and requires further treatment.

A premature chronology might say:

"The doctor negligently discharged the patient."

A proper investigative chronology would instead ask:

Presentation
What symptoms did the patient report?

Assessment
What did the practitioner find?

Investigations
What investigations were performed or considered?

Results
What information was available?

Decision
Why was the patient discharged?

Instructions
What was the patient told to do after discharge?

Subsequent events
What happened after the patient left?

Return
When did the patient return and what was the condition then?

This approach preserves the facts and leaves the legal conclusion to the later analysis.

SCA A M and Another v MEC for Health, Western Cape

[2020] ZASCA 89

The case concerned a trauma patient's discharge after assessment and the subsequent question whether the decision met the required standard of care. It illustrates why the decision itself must be reconstructed before negligence is concluded.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner's Checklist

The Practitioner's Test

Before concluding that a clinical decision was negligent, ask:

  • What exactly was the decision?
  • Who made it?
  • What did that person know at the time?
  • What information was unavailable?
  • What findings supported the decision?
  • What evidence suggests that another course should have been followed?
  • Is that conclusion based on evidence or hindsight?
  • What expert question must be answered?
Closing Principle
The patient's outcome may tell you where the investigation should look. It does not, by itself, tell you whether the earlier clinical decision was negligent.
