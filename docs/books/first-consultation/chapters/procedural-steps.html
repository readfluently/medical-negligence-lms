Procedural Steps

A medical-negligence matter does not move directly from a patient's complaint to a trial.

There is an investigative stage, a pre-litigation stage and then a formal litigation process. Each stage has its own purpose.

Practitioner's Principle
Do not confuse:

investigating a possible claim with instituting legal proceedings.

The quality of the litigation often depends on the quality of the investigation that came before it.

Before Litigation: Build the File

The first stage is not necessarily the issuing of summons.

The practitioner should first establish the factual and documentary foundation of the proposed claim.

Pre-Litigation Investigation

The investigation comes first

A client may arrive saying:

"The doctor was negligent and I want to sue."

That statement identifies an allegation. It does not establish the claim.

The practitioner should establish:

  • what happened;
  • when it happened;
  • where it happened;
  • who was involved;
  • what the patient's condition was beforehand;
  • what treatment occurred;
  • what injury followed;
  • what evidence exists;
  • what remains unknown; and
  • what legal and procedural deadlines may apply.
The objective is not to make the case look stronger.

The objective is to find out whether the evidence supports the case at all.

Institution of Proceedings

Investigation is not institution

Legal proceedings are instituted through the procedures required by the applicable court rules.

The procedural mechanism depends on the forum and nature of the claim.

The practitioner should therefore identify:

  • the appropriate court;
  • jurisdiction;
  • correct defendants;
  • cause or causes of action;
  • applicable prescription position;
  • statutory notice requirements where applicable;
  • necessary initiating process;
  • correct service.
"We are going to sue" is not the same procedural event as "proceedings have been instituted".

Pleadings

Plead the material facts

Pleadings define the factual and legal dispute between the parties.

Rule 18(4) of the Uniform Rules requires a pleading to contain a clear and concise statement of the material facts upon which the pleader relies, with sufficient particularity to enable the opposing party to reply.

Uniform Rules Rule 18(4)

The rule requires material facts to be pleaded with sufficient particularity to enable the opposing party to respond.

Consult the Uniform Rules →

Medical-negligence pleading questions

  • What duty or relationship existed?
  • What conduct is alleged?
  • What standard is relied upon?
  • What departure is alleged?
  • What injury is alleged?
  • What causal connection is alleged?
  • What loss is claimed?
Pleadings should emerge from the investigation. The investigation should not be reconstructed merely to support a pleading that has already been drafted.

Discovery

Discovery is evidence mapping

Discovery is not merely an administrative exchange of documents. It allows the parties to identify documentary material relevant to the issues in dispute.

Under Uniform Rule 35(1), a party may require discovery on oath of documents and tape recordings relating to matters in question, subject to the rule's requirements.

Uniform Rules Rule 35(1)

The current rule provides for discovery on oath and generally allows 20 days for compliance after a proper notice.

Consult Rule 35 →

Medical-negligence discovery

The practitioner should think beyond "the hospital file".

  • admission records;
  • clinical notes;
  • nursing records;
  • theatre records;
  • anaesthetic records;
  • medication charts;
  • imaging;
  • laboratory results;
  • referral records;
  • electronic communications where relevant;
  • subsequent treatment records;
  • relevant expert material.
High Court Nteleko obo Nteleko v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape Province

[2023] ZAECMHC 21

The judgment records the operation of Rule 35 discovery and the mechanism available where a party considers that further discovery is required.

Expert Evidence Procedure

Expert evidence is procedural as well as substantive

The practitioner must distinguish:

Selecting the expert
An investigative decision.

Obtaining the opinion
An evidentiary step.

Giving notice of the intention to call the expert
A procedural step.

Delivering the required summary and reasons
A further procedural step.

Current High Court Rule 36(9)

Under the current Uniform Rules, where the plaintiff intends to call an expert, the notice of intention to call the expert is generally due not more than 30 days after close of pleadings.

Where the defendant intends to call the expert, the corresponding period is generally not more than 60 days after close of pleadings.

Rule 36(9)(b) then provides for delivery of the plaintiff's expert summary generally within 90 days and the defendant's within 120 days after close of pleadings, subject to the rule's case-management proviso.

Uniform Rules — Current Rule 36(9)

Notice and summary must comply with the applicable time periods and, in any event, be delivered before the first case-management conference or as directed by the case-management judge.

Consult Rule 36 →
Never treat expert evidence as something that can simply be added shortly before trial without checking the procedural rules and applicable case-management directives.

Case Management

The modern High Court process

Current High Court procedure includes formal case-management mechanisms designed to improve trial readiness and narrow the issues.

Rule 37A requires the parties to address, among other matters, discovery, expert evidence, witnesses and outstanding issues.

Uniform Rules — Rule 37A Case Management

The current Rule 37A framework requires attention to matters including discovery, expert witnesses, witnesses, admissions and the steps required to bring the case to trial readiness.

Consult Rule 37A →

Practitioner preparation

  • Identify what is admitted.
  • Identify what remains disputed.
  • Identify outstanding discovery.
  • Identify expert issues.
  • Identify witnesses.
  • Identify possible admissions.
  • Identify matters that can be narrowed.
  • Identify outstanding steps necessary for trial readiness.

Trial

The trial is the end of a long evidence-building process

By the time the medical-negligence matter reaches trial, the practitioner should already understand:

  • the factual chronology;
  • the pleaded case;
  • the evidence supporting each material fact;
  • the expert disputes;
  • the causation issues;
  • the damages evidence;
  • the admissions;
  • the unresolved factual disputes.
Trial preparation should not be the first time the practitioner discovers what the case is about.

The Investigator → Litigator Transition
Early in the matter:

Find out what happened.

Then:

Determine what the evidence supports.

Then:

Formulate the legal case.

Then:

Conduct the litigation according to the applicable rules.

The Practitioner's Test

Before progressing from one procedural stage to the next, ask:

  • What stage is the matter currently in?
  • What must be completed before the next stage?
  • Which deadline governs?
  • What document proves that the step was completed?
  • What happens if the step is missed?
  • Has the prescription position changed?
  • Has the evidence changed?
  • Has the pleaded case changed?
Closing Principle
Good procedural practice is not merely knowing the rules.

It is knowing:

what stage the matter is at,
what must happen next,
by when,
and what evidence proves that it happened.
Procedural framework: This chapter principally uses the current South African High Court Uniform Rules as the procedural framework. Magistrates' Court proceedings have their own rules and should not automatically be treated as identical to High Court proceedings.

Current High Court examples: Rule 18 deals with pleadings; Rule 35 deals with discovery; Rule 36 deals with examinations and expert evidence; Rule 36(9) provides current post-pleading expert-notice and summary periods; and Rule 37A deals with case management. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Important: Procedural rules, court directives and case-management orders can change the timetable applicable to a particular matter. The current rules and applicable court directives should therefore be checked before relying on any procedural deadline in a live case.
