Statutory Notice

A medical-negligence claim against a private practitioner or private hospital is not dealt with in exactly the same procedural way as a claim against an organ of state.

Where the proposed defendant is an organ of state, the Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 may impose an additional statutory notice requirement.

Practitioner's Principle
Do not treat statutory notice as "another form of prescription".

It is a separate procedural requirement that must be investigated alongside, and not confused with, prescription.

First Question: Is the Defendant an Organ of State?

Before calculating a notice deadline, determine whether the proposed defendant falls within the statutory definition of an organ of state.

This question should be answered for each potential defendant separately.

A medical-negligence matter may involve several defendants, for example:

  • a provincial Department of Health;
  • a public hospital;
  • an individual medical practitioner;
  • a private hospital;
  • another healthcare institution.
The fact that one defendant is an organ of state does not automatically make every other defendant an organ of state.

Section 3: Notice of Intended Legal Proceedings

Section 3(1) generally provides that legal proceedings for recovery of a debt may not be instituted against an organ of state unless:

  • the creditor has given the organ of state written notice of the intention to institute the proceedings; or
  • the organ of state has consented in writing to proceedings without notice or on the basis of a defective notice.

The Six-Month Notice Period

Section 3(2)(a) generally requires the notice to be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due.

The practitioner must therefore establish the date on which the debt became due before calculating the six-month notice deadline.

This is why the statutory-notice investigation cannot be separated completely from the prescription investigation.

When Is the Debt "Due" for Statutory Notice?

Section 3(3)(a) contains a specific formulation for purposes of the notice period.

A debt may not be regarded as due until the creditor has knowledge of:

  • the identity of the organ of state; and
  • the facts giving rise to the debt.

The creditor is nevertheless regarded as having acquired that knowledge when it could have been acquired through the exercise of reasonable care, unless the organ of state wilfully prevented the acquisition of that knowledge.

Constitutional Court President of the Republic of South Africa and Another v Tembani and Others

[2024] ZACC 5; 2025 (2) SA 371 (CC)

The Constitutional Court noted that sections 12(2) and 12(3) of the Prescription Act substantially mirror the formulation contained in section 3(3)(a) of the State Organs Act.

Statutory Notice Versus Prescription

PRESCRIPTION

The question is generally:
When did the debt become due, and when will the applicable prescription period expire?


STATUTORY NOTICE

The question is:
Has the required notice to the organ of state been served within the statutory period?
Remember
A six-month notice deadline and a three-year prescription period are two different legal requirements.

What Must the Notice Contain?

Section 3(2)(b) requires the notice to briefly set out:

  • the facts giving rise to the debt; and
  • such particulars of the debt as are within the creditor's knowledge.

The notice should therefore not be treated as a vague announcement that litigation may occur.

The practitioner should ensure that the notice identifies the factual basis of the intended claim with sufficient clarity for the statutory purpose.

Practical Notice Content

Although the statutory formulation should always be checked directly, the practitioner should ordinarily ensure that the notice addresses, where applicable:

  • the identity of the claimant;
  • the organ of state against which proceedings are contemplated;
  • the relevant medical event;
  • the place of treatment;
  • the relevant dates;
  • the healthcare providers involved;
  • the material facts giving rise to the claim; and
  • the nature or particulars of the debt within the claimant's knowledge.
The notice is not the place to guess at facts that have not yet been established. It should accurately state the material information within the creditor's knowledge.

Service of the Notice

A correctly drafted notice can still create problems if it is served on the wrong person or by an unauthorised method.

Section 4 regulates service of the notice.

Depending on the identity of the organ of state, the Act specifies the appropriate recipient and provides recognised methods of service.

Legislation State Organs Act — Section 4

The Act provides for service by methods including delivery by hand, certified mail and, subject to the statutory conditions, electronic mail or facsimile.

Read section 4 on SAFLII →
Practitioner rule
Never treat proof of drafting as proof of service.

Preserve the evidence showing:

who received it,
when it was received,
how it was served,
and why that method and recipient complied with section 4.

Notice Is Not the Institution of Legal Proceedings

One of the most important distinctions in the file is the difference between giving notice and instituting proceedings.

The statutory notice alerts the organ of state to the intended claim. It does not itself constitute the ordinary initiating process by which the action is instituted.

This matters because a practitioner must not assume that serving the notice alone has solved the prescription problem.

Recent High Court authority Ngwane v Minister of Police and Another

[2025] ZAWCHC 459

The court held that a section 3 notice is a procedural prerequisite to proceedings against an organ of state and is not itself the judicial interruption of prescription contemplated by section 15 of the Prescription Act.

The 60-Day Rule

Section 5(2) of the State Organs Act provides that, generally, process may not be served before the expiry of 60 days after the notice has been served.

The Act contains an important exception: if the organ of state repudiates liability in writing before the expiry of the period, the creditor may proceed after that written repudiation.

What If Notice Was Not Served Within Six Months?

A late notice does not necessarily end the matter.

Section 3(4) permits the creditor to apply for condonation where the organ of state relies upon the failure to comply with the notice requirement.

The Three Condonation Requirements

1. The debt has not been extinguished by prescription

↓

2. Good cause exists for the failure

↓

3. The organ of state was not unreasonably prejudiced
SCA Madinda v Minister of Safety and Security

[2008] ZASCA 34; 2008 (4) SA 312 (SCA)

The SCA explained the structure of the section 3(4) enquiry and emphasised the need to consider all three statutory requirements. Good cause and absence of unreasonable prejudice are distinct considerations.

Read Madinda on SAFLII →

What Does "Good Cause" Require?

A condonation application should explain the circumstances surrounding the failure to serve a timeous notice.

The court considers the facts of the particular matter rather than applying a mechanical formula.

Relevant material may include:

  • the explanation for the delay;
  • the length of the delay;
  • the conduct of the claimant;
  • the conduct of legal representatives where relevant;
  • the strength of the proposed claim;
  • the steps taken to investigate the claim.
SCA Madinda v Minister of Safety and Security

The SCA explained that the merits may have relevance to the condonation enquiry and that the court must consider the statutory requirements as a whole.

Read the judgment →

Prejudice to the Organ of State

The statute specifically requires the court to consider whether the organ of state was not unreasonably prejudiced by the failure to serve the notice within the required period.

The practitioner should therefore investigate what happened to the evidence during the period of delay.

Evidence-related questions

  • Are the relevant records still available?
  • Are witnesses still identifiable?
  • Are witnesses still available?
  • Can the clinical sequence still be reconstructed?
  • Has any important evidence been lost?
  • Has the organ of state identified any actual prejudice?

Recent Medical-Negligence Application

High Court — 2026 Badenhorst and Others v MEC for Health, North West Province

[2026] ZANWHC 168

This was a medical-negligence matter involving alleged negligent treatment by employees of a provincial health department. The applicants sought condonation for failure to serve the statutory notice within the prescribed period.

The court considered the interaction between section 3 of the State Organs Act and section 12(3) of the Prescription Act and ultimately granted condonation after finding that the statutory requirements had been satisfied on the facts.

The decision should be understood as a fact-specific application of the legislation, not as a general rule that a particular complaint or investigation automatically postpones prescription or notice.

The Practitioner's Test

Before moving forward with proceedings against an organ of state, ask:

  • Have I identified the correct organ of state?
  • Have I calculated the six-month notice deadline?
  • Have I separated that deadline from prescription?
  • Is the notice compliant with section 3?
  • Was the notice served in the correct way?
  • Can service be proved?
  • Have I checked the 60-day period?
  • Has written repudiation changed the position?
  • Has the underlying debt remained within prescription?
  • If notice was late, have I investigated condonation immediately?
Closing Principle
Statutory notice is not merely a letter before summons.

It is a statutory procedural requirement with its own deadline, service rules and consequences of non-compliance.

Treat the notice date, the prescription date and the litigation date as separate events in the chronology.
Primary legislation: Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002.

Key authorities: Madinda v Minister of Safety and Security; Minister of Safety and Security v De Witt; President of the Republic of South Africa and Another v Tembani and Others; and selected later High Court authorities including Badenhorst and Ngwane.

Important: Statutory notice is highly fact-specific. This chapter is a practitioner research aid and does not replace checking the current Act, applicable regulations, service requirements, pleadings and current case law in an individual matter.
