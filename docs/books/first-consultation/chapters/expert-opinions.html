Expert Opinions

Expert evidence is often where a medical-negligence investigation moves from the patient's story and medical records into specialised questions that ultimately have to be proved.

Practitioner's principle: Do not begin by asking, “Which expert should I appoint?” Begin by asking, “What proposition requires specialised knowledge?”

The practitioner should identify the question before selecting the expert. At the first consultation, this means identifying potential areas of expertise rather than immediately commissioning reports.

Start With the Question

The expert process should begin with a precise question rather than a generic request for “a medical opinion”.

Was the patient's presentation consistent with condition X?

What investigation would reasonably have been indicated?

Should the patient have been referred?

Was the treatment appropriate?

Would earlier intervention probably have altered the outcome?

What is the patient's present prognosis?

The Question Requiring Specialised Knowledge

The starting point is the proposition that cannot responsibly be answered through ordinary legal analysis alone.

The practitioner should identify the precise medical or technical question before deciding who is best qualified to answer it.

“Was the doctor negligent?” may be too broad as an instruction. More useful questions may include:

  • What symptoms were clinically significant?
  • What investigations were reasonably indicated?
  • What diagnosis should reasonably have been considered?
  • What treatment options were available?
  • What risks and benefits attached to those options?
  • Would earlier intervention probably have changed the outcome?

Once the specialised proposition has been identified, the practitioner can determine which discipline is capable of addressing it.

See Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another [2001] ZASCA 12 on the evaluation of expert opinions and logical reasoning.

Potential Expert

A potential expert is not simply a medical practitioner whose specialty appears relevant to the case.

The more useful starting point is the proposition that requires specialised knowledge.

Consider neurological injury following surgery. The case may generate several distinct questions:

  • What happened during the operation?
  • Was anaesthetic management appropriate?
  • What neurological injury occurred?
  • When did the injury probably occur?
  • Could earlier intervention have prevented or reduced it?
  • What are the long-term functional consequences?

Those questions may not all belong to one expert.

The appropriate expert or expert team depends upon the propositions that must be established.

Practitioner question:

Before choosing an expert, write down the exact proposition that expert must address.

Expert Evidence

Expert evidence is intended to assist the court on matters requiring specialised knowledge outside ordinary judicial experience.

An expert does not decide the litigation. The court remains responsible for evaluating the evidence and reaching the legal conclusion.

In Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic , the Supreme Court of Appeal emphasised the importance of evaluating the reasoning underlying competing expert opinions.

Key proposition: The court does not merely choose the expert who sounds more convincing. It evaluates the factual foundation and logic of the competing opinions.

Expert Independence and Objectivity

An expert may be instructed by one of the parties, but that does not turn the expert into an advocate.

The expert should provide an objective opinion based upon the specialised knowledge of the expert and the available material.

In Schneider NO and Others v Aspeling and Another [2010] ZAWCHC 3 , the court dealt extensively with the responsibilities of an expert witness, including independence, objectivity and identification of the factual basis of the opinion.

Practitioner test:

Would the report remain professionally defensible if it were read by the opposing party?

Factual Basis

An expert's conclusion depends upon the facts, records and assumptions from which the reasoning begins.

The practitioner should ask:

  • What records did the expert receive?
  • Which facts did the expert assume?
  • Which facts are actually established?
  • Are important records missing?
  • Does the expert distinguish fact from assumption?
  • Would the opinion change if a disputed fact were resolved differently?

In Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 , the Supreme Court of Appeal addressed the importance of the reasoning and foundation underlying expert evidence.

Standard of Care

The standard of care asks what level of skill and care could reasonably be expected in the relevant circumstances and professional field.

The expert should not simply say: “I would have done something different.”

The opinion should explain:

  • the clinical circumstances;
  • the available options;
  • the relevant risks and benefits;
  • what a reasonably competent practitioner would have considered;
  • what actually occurred; and
  • why the conduct did or did not fall below the relevant standard.

The approach in Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic remains central to the evaluation of expert opinion in medical negligence litigation.

Expert Evidence on Causation

An expert who is well placed to discuss treatment may not necessarily be the person best placed to answer every causation question.

Separate questions may include:

  • What caused the injury?
  • When did the injury probably occur?
  • Did the alleged breach contribute to the injury?
  • Was there another probable cause?
  • Would earlier treatment probably have changed the outcome?

The practitioner must therefore identify exactly which causation proposition requires specialised evidence.

See Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape [2015] ZACC 33 concerning medical negligence, expert evidence and causation.

Expert Instruction

A vague instruction is likely to produce a vague report.

The instruction should identify the clinical history, relevant records and questions requiring specialised opinion without dictating the answer.

Was the presentation consistent with the diagnosis later made?

What investigations would reasonably have been considered at that stage?

Was referral indicated?

Was the treatment appropriate in the circumstances?

What, in your opinion, caused the injury?

Would earlier intervention probably have altered the outcome?

Good instruction: identifies the proposition to be answered.

Poor instruction: tells the expert what conclusion the practitioner wants.

Material Supplied to the Expert

The practitioner should not assume that the hospital file is necessarily the complete evidential foundation.

Depending upon the case, the expert may require:

  • hospital records;
  • nursing notes;
  • theatre records;
  • anaesthetic records;
  • laboratory results;
  • radiology reports and images;
  • previous medical records;
  • subsequent treatment records;
  • rehabilitation records; and
  • relevant witness information.
Do not hide difficult material. A contradictory record, adverse finding or relevant pre-existing condition may affect the reliability of the opinion and should be properly considered.

Medico-Legal Report

A useful report should allow the court to understand the expert's expertise, information considered, assumptions, reasoning and conclusions.

The practitioner should ask:

  • What question was the expert asked?
  • What material was considered?
  • What facts were assumed?
  • What principles were applied?
  • What alternative explanations were considered?
  • Why was one explanation preferred?
  • What limitations affect the opinion?

In Schneider , the court emphasised the importance of the expert identifying the factual foundation and limitations of the opinion.

Rule 36(9)

Rule 36(9) of the Uniform Rules of Court regulates expert witnesses and the procedural delivery of expert notices and summaries in High Court litigation.

The current procedural timetable must always be checked against the applicable rules, directives and case-management order.

Expert Notice

The procedural notice concerning the intention to call an expert witness.

The practitioner should diarise the applicable deadline immediately and check whether the case-management order imposes a different or earlier timetable.

Expert Summary

The summary communicates the substance of the expert's proposed evidence and the reasons for the opinion.

It should accurately identify the material opinions on which the party intends to rely.

Important: Do not assume that a historical deadline remains current. Check the current Uniform Rules, applicable court, practice directives and case-management order.

Joint Minutes

Where experts for the opposing parties address the same issues, they may be required to meet and identify matters of agreement and disagreement.

The joint minute should make the remaining dispute clearer, not obscure it.

The practitioner should identify:

  • what the experts agree upon;
  • what remains disputed;
  • why they disagree; and
  • whether the disagreement concerns fact, interpretation or opinion.

See Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 concerning the importance of expert joint minutes.

Agreed Factual Proposition

An agreed factual proposition is an underlying factual matter that the experts accept as common ground for purposes of their analysis.

The practitioner should still verify the evidential foundation of the agreement.

An apparent agreement is only useful if the practitioner understands precisely what has been agreed and what remains outside the agreement.

Agreed Expert Opinion

An agreed expert opinion is a conclusion accepted by the relevant experts after consideration of the material.

Such agreement may substantially narrow the issues for trial.

The practitioner should nevertheless determine precisely what proposition has been agreed and what the court still needs to decide.

See Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 .

Conflicting Experts

A medical-negligence case may involve genuinely competing professional opinions.

The court must evaluate the reasoning underlying those opinions rather than simply choosing the expert with the stronger title or more confident presentation.

The foundational authority remains Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

The practitioner should therefore ask:

  • What facts support each opinion?
  • What assumptions differ?
  • What methodology differs?
  • What alternative explanation has each expert considered?
  • Which opinion better fits the objective evidence?

Unfavourable Opinion

An unfavourable opinion is information about the strength of the case. It should be analysed before deciding what strategy follows.

Ask:

  • Is the expert answering the correct question?
  • Was the complete medical record available?
  • Are the factual assumptions correct?
  • Was contrary evidence considered?
  • Is the conclusion stronger than the reasoning supports?
  • Is the disagreement actually about law rather than medicine?
Practitioner discipline: The purpose of expert evidence is to discover what the evidence supports, not to manufacture an opinion that supports the client.

Expert Team

A complex injury may involve several specialised questions that cannot responsibly be answered by one discipline.

Question Possible expertise
Was the surgery appropriately performed? Relevant surgical specialist
Was anaesthetic management appropriate? Anaesthetist
What injury occurred? Relevant clinical specialist
When did the injury probably occur? Relevant clinical specialist
What is the functional impact? Rehabilitation or occupational expertise
What is the vocational consequence? Industrial psychology
How should future loss be quantified? Actuarial expertise

Each expert should have a defined role and a defined proposition to address.

Preparing the Expert for Trial

A strong written report does not automatically produce strong oral evidence.

Trial preparation should ensure that the expert understands:

  • the records;
  • the factual assumptions;
  • the limits of the opinion;
  • the opposing expert's position; and
  • the remaining areas of disagreement.

What is this expert actually proving?

What evidence supports that proposition?

What is the strongest argument against it?

The preparation should not turn the expert into an advocate. The expert remains responsible for providing an independent professional opinion.

Reasoning Behind the Opinion

The expert's conclusion should not appear as an unexplained assertion.

The court must be able to understand the route by which the expert moved from the facts and specialised knowledge to the ultimate opinion.

This is why the practitioner should scrutinise not only the conclusion but the reasoning that leads to it.

This principle is central to Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

Forensic Purpose

A forensic purpose is the specific reason an item of expert evidence is needed in the litigation.

Before incurring an expert cost, the practitioner should be able to identify exactly what proposition the report is intended to establish or test.

The question is not: “Does this type of case usually have this expert?”

The better question is: “What issue in this case requires this expert's evidence?”

Expert Question Test

Before appointing an expert, complete this sentence:

“I need this expert because the court must decide whether ______.”

Then complete:

“The expert will assist the court by explaining ______.”

If neither sentence can be completed clearly, the practitioner may not yet have identified the issue requiring expert evidence.

Practitioner's Expert File

Before the expert evidence is finalised

  • The specialised proposition has been identified.
  • The expert's discipline is appropriate.
  • The expert's qualifications and experience have been considered.
  • The relevant records have been supplied.
  • Material adverse information has not been hidden.
  • The instruction identifies the questions requiring an opinion.
  • The report identifies material assumptions.
  • The reasoning supporting the conclusions is understood.
  • Limitations and uncertainty have been identified.
  • Applicable expert-evidence rules have been considered.
  • The opposing expert report has been reviewed.
  • The joint minute has been reviewed.
  • Areas of agreement and disagreement are clear.
  • The expert evidence fits into the pleaded case.

Interactive Exercise: Choose the Expert

Scenario

A patient undergoes surgery and later develops severe neurological impairment.

Possible experts include:

  • the surgeon;
  • the anaesthetist;
  • the neurologist;
  • the radiologist;
  • the rehabilitation specialist;
  • the occupational therapist;
  • the industrial psychologist; and
  • the actuary.

Question 1

Which expert would address whether the surgery itself was appropriately performed?

Reveal discussion

The relevant surgical discipline would ordinarily address that proposition. The precise specialty depends upon the procedure in issue.

Question 2

Which expert would address whether anaesthetic management was appropriate?

Reveal discussion

An anaesthetist would ordinarily be the relevant discipline for questions concerning anaesthetic management.

Question 3

Which expert might address the neurological injury and its consequences?

Reveal discussion

A neurologist or another appropriately qualified specialist may be required, depending upon the exact injury and proposition in issue.

Question 4

Which experts may be required to address future functional consequences and loss of earning capacity?

Reveal discussion

More than one discipline may be appropriate. Functional consequences may require rehabilitation or occupational expertise; vocational consequences may require industrial psychology; and quantification may require actuarial expertise.

The Final Practitioner Test

Do not appoint an expert because the case “needs an expert.”

Appoint an expert because a specific proposition requires specialised knowledge and that proposition matters to the outcome of the case.

The expert should receive the material necessary to answer that proposition independently and objectively.

The report should explain the reasoning.

The opposing opinion should be tested.

The joint minute should narrow the issues.

Before trial, the practitioner should know exactly what each expert is there to prove.

Research note: Expert-evidence procedure should always be checked against the current applicable rules, directives and case-management order. High Court and Magistrates' Court procedures are not interchangeable.
