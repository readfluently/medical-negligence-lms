The Development of the Injury

A medical-negligence investigation should not begin with the patient's final condition and work backwards.

It should establish the patient's condition before the relevant medical event, identify what changed, and then trace the development of the condition through the subsequent medical history.

Practitioner's Principle
Before asking what caused the injury, establish precisely what the injury was, when it appeared, and how it developed.

Start Before the Alleged Negligence

One of the most important steps is to establish the patient's pre-treatment condition.

The purpose is not merely to obtain background information. The patient's condition before the medical event may later become important when assessing the alleged breach, causation, the extent of the injury and the consequences flowing from it.

Pre-treatment condition

What was the patient's condition before the treatment complained of?

Post-event condition

What changed after the relevant medical event?

Pre-Treatment Condition

The starting point

The practitioner should establish what the patient was like before the medical event complained of.

The relevant history may include:

  • previous diagnoses;
  • previous injuries;
  • previous operations;
  • chronic conditions;
  • medication;
  • previous symptoms;
  • previous treatment;
  • previous investigations;
  • previous admissions;
  • physical functioning;
  • employment or education;
  • independence and activities of daily living.

Why it matters

The pre-treatment condition may assist in distinguishing an existing condition from a new injury and may later become relevant to causation and the assessment of loss.

Two questions
What was the patient's condition before the event?

What changed afterwards?

Onset of the Injury

Identify the earliest evidence

The practitioner should identify when the alleged injury first became apparent or was first recorded.

Do not assume that the date on which the injury was formally diagnosed is necessarily the date on which the injury first existed.

Questions

  • When did the patient first experience the symptom?
  • When did somebody else first observe the change?
  • When was the change first reported to a healthcare provider?
  • When was it first documented?
  • When was it first investigated?
  • When was a diagnosis made?
The first diagnosis and the first manifestation of the condition are not necessarily the same date.

Progression of the Injury

Trace the change

The injury should be tracked through the subsequent medical history rather than recorded simply as a final diagnosis.

The chronology may need to identify:

  • initial symptoms;
  • changes in severity;
  • new symptoms;
  • functional decline;
  • new diagnoses;
  • new investigations;
  • additional treatment;
  • referrals;
  • hospitalisation;
  • rehabilitation;
  • permanent consequences.

Why progression matters

A progressive condition may raise different investigative questions from an injury that occurred immediately following a particular medical event.

The chronology should therefore distinguish:

Immediate change
The condition appears shortly after the event.

Delayed change
The condition becomes apparent later.

Progressive change
The condition develops or worsens over time.

Alternative Causes

Do not stop at the client's theory

A patient may believe that the medical treatment caused the injury. That allegation should be recorded and investigated, but the investigation should also identify other possible explanations.

Consider:

  • pre-existing disease;
  • natural progression of disease;
  • another medical event;
  • subsequent treatment;
  • trauma;
  • infection;
  • complications unrelated to the alleged breach;
  • another healthcare provider's intervention.
Identifying an alternative explanation does not decide the case. It identifies a question that must be tested against the evidence.

The Causal Link

Injury is not enough

Even where a healthcare provider acted negligently, the practitioner must still investigate whether that conduct caused the harm complained of.

SCA AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape

[2019] ZASCA 102

The SCA emphasised that wrongful and negligent conduct does not by itself establish liability. The plaintiff must still establish the required causal link between the conduct and the harm.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Do not reason backwards

The fact that the patient suffered an injury does not, without more, establish that the healthcare provider caused it or that the provider was negligent.

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

[2018] ZASCA 132

The SCA held that the plaintiff bears the onus of establishing the damage-causing event and cautioned against impermissibly reasoning backwards from the effect to the cause.

Practitioner questions

  • What exactly is the alleged injury?
  • What event is alleged to have caused it?
  • When did the injury occur?
  • What evidence connects the event to the injury?
  • What alternative causes exist?
  • What expert question must be answered?

Hindsight

The final condition can distort the investigation

Once a serious injury has occurred, it can appear obvious that an earlier medical decision must have been wrong.

The practitioner should resist treating the eventual outcome as proof of what should have been done earlier.

Instead, reconstruct the clinical sequence:

What was known then?

↓

What was observed then?

↓

What decision was made then?

↓

What happened afterwards?
The eventual injury may identify the issue to investigate. It does not automatically establish the answer.

Injury Versus Loss

Do not collapse the two

The medical injury is not necessarily the same thing as every consequence that follows from the injury.

For example, a physical injury may result in:

  • pain;
  • loss of function;
  • disability;
  • additional treatment;
  • need for care;
  • loss of earnings;
  • changes in education;
  • changes in daily activities.

The practitioner should therefore distinguish:

The injury
What medical harm occurred?

The consequences
What happened because of that harm?

The loss
What legally compensable loss is alleged?

Building the Injury Timeline

A useful injury timeline should move from the patient's baseline condition through the medical event and into the later consequences.

1. Baseline
Patient's condition before the relevant medical event.

↓

2. Medical event
Treatment, procedure, consultation, admission or other relevant event.

↓

3. First change
Earliest symptom, sign or functional change.

↓

4. Recognition
When the change was reported, observed or recorded.

↓

5. Investigation
Tests, imaging, referrals or other investigation.

↓

6. Diagnosis
When the condition was diagnosed or characterised.

↓

7. Subsequent treatment
Treatment received after the injury emerged.

↓

8. Current condition
The patient's present medical and functional condition.

Follow the Injury Through the Records

For every important change in the patient's condition, ask what documentary evidence should exist.

  • clinical notes
  • nursing notes
  • vital-sign records
  • laboratory results
  • imaging and radiology
  • operative records
  • medication charts
  • referral letters
  • discharge records
  • rehabilitation records
  • subsequent specialist records
Records question
If this change in the patient's condition occurred, what record should exist showing when it happened?

The Patient's Account

The client's account is an important starting point, but it should be separated from assumptions and legal conclusions.

For example:

Client's statement
"After the operation, my leg stopped working."

The practitioner should convert that statement into factual questions:

When did the patient first notice weakness?

Was the leg completely or partially affected?

Who first observed the change?

Was the change recorded by nursing staff?

When was the treating doctor informed?

What examination was performed?

What treatment followed?

What did subsequent records show?
A client's description should generate factual questions. It should not prematurely determine the legal conclusion.

When the Injury Requires Expert Analysis

Some questions concerning the injury cannot be answered through ordinary factual evidence alone.

The practitioner should identify the specialised question rather than simply deciding that an expert is needed.

Possible specialised questions

What mechanism could have produced the injury?

Was the injury consistent with the alleged event?

Could the injury have resulted from another cause?

When would the injury probably have occurred?

Would earlier intervention probably have changed the outcome?

What is the patient's present prognosis?
Begin with the question requiring specialised knowledge. Select the appropriate expert only after the question is identified.

How the Courts Approach Injury and Causation

SCA AN v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape

[2019] ZASCA 102

The SCA separated the questions of negligence and factual causation. Even where negligent conduct has been established, the claimant must still establish that the conduct caused or materially contributed to the harm.

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

[2018] ZASCA 132

The SCA warned that an injury does not itself prove the damage-causing event, negligence or causation. The investigation must identify and prove the relevant damage-causing occurrence.

The Practitioner's Test

Before concluding that a particular medical act caused the alleged injury, ask:

  • What was the patient's condition before the event?
  • What precisely is the alleged injury?
  • When did it first appear?
  • How did it develop?
  • What evidence establishes its progression?
  • What alternative causes exist?
  • What evidence links the medical event to the injury?
  • What questions require expert evidence?
  • What consequences followed from the injury?
Closing Principle
First establish the injury.

Then establish its development.

Then investigate its cause.

Do not reverse that order simply because the eventual outcome is obvious.
