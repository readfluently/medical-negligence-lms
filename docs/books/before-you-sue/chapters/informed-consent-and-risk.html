Informed Consent and Risk

Consent is one of the most frequently misunderstood issues in medical-negligence litigation.

A signed consent form does not automatically mean that the practitioner is protected from liability. Equally, the fact that a recognised risk materialised does not automatically establish negligence.

Practitioner principle: The investigation must distinguish between the patient's agreement to undergo treatment, the patient's informed acceptance of material risks, the quality of the treatment itself, and the legal consequences of a risk that was disclosed and accepted.

1. Consent Is About Patient Autonomy

Medical treatment involves an intervention into the patient's body. The law therefore recognises the patient's right to make decisions concerning medical treatment and bodily integrity.

Consent is not merely a formality by which a patient authorises a practitioner to perform a procedure. The patient must have sufficient information to make a meaningful decision about whether to undergo the proposed treatment.

This is why the concept of informed consent is different from simply obtaining a signature.

2. The National Health Act

The National Health Act 61 of 2003 provides an important statutory framework for informed consent.

Section 6 requires a healthcare provider to inform a user of relevant information concerning the health service, including the user's health status, the range of diagnostic procedures and treatment options generally available, the benefits, risks, costs and consequences generally associated with each option, and the user's right to refuse health services.

Section 6(2) further requires that, where possible, the information be provided in a language the user understands and in a manner that takes account of the user's level of literacy.

Section 7 provides that a health service may generally not be provided without the user's informed consent, subject to specified exceptions. The Act also requires the healthcare provider to take all reasonable steps to obtain informed consent.

Sections 6 and 7 are particularly important when investigating whether consent was properly informed and whether the circumstances fell within an exception to the ordinary consent requirement.

3. A Consent Form Is Evidence — Not the Entire Enquiry

A signed consent form can be important evidence. It may establish that a patient was informed of particular matters and agreed to the proposed procedure.

But the investigation should not stop at the form.

The practitioner should ask:

  • What information was actually communicated?
  • Who communicated it?
  • When was it communicated?
  • Was there enough time for the patient to consider it?
  • Did the patient understand the information?
  • Was the material risk identified?
  • Were reasonable alternatives discussed where relevant?
  • Was the patient given an opportunity to ask questions?
  • Was the consent voluntary?
  • Was the consent limited to a particular procedure or intervention?

4. Castell v De Greef

The leading South African authority on informed consent is Castell v De Greef.

The case represents an important movement away from a purely practitioner-centred approach to disclosure and towards recognition of patient autonomy.

The enquiry is not simply whether a doctor thought a particular risk was worth mentioning. The patient's right to make an informed decision about medical treatment is central to the analysis.

Castell v De Greef

The case established important principles concerning informed consent, material risk and the information that must be provided to a patient before medical intervention.

5. What Is a Material Risk?

Not every conceivable risk of treatment must necessarily be explained to a patient in exhaustive detail.

The important question is whether the risk was material in the circumstances.

In the South African approach associated with Castell v De Greef, a risk is material where, in the circumstances of the particular case, a reasonable person in the patient's position would be likely to attach significance to it, or where the practitioner knows or reasonably should know that the particular patient would be likely to attach significance to it.

The significance of the risk therefore cannot always be determined solely by asking how frequently it occurs.

Important: A relatively uncommon risk may still be material if its consequences are sufficiently serious or if the practitioner knows that the particular patient would attach significance to it.

6. Knowledge, Appreciation and Consent

The courts have identified several requirements for valid informed consent.

The patient must have knowledge of the nature and extent of the relevant harm or risk, appreciate and understand the nature and extent of that risk, consent to the relevant harm or assume the risk, and give consent that is sufficiently comprehensive for the transaction in question.

This distinction is important because merely telling a patient that "there are risks" does not necessarily establish informed consent.

Louwrens v Oldwage

The Supreme Court of Appeal considered the requirements for informed consent and referred to the principles developed in Castell v De Greef.

7. Consent to the Risk Is Not Consent to Negligence

This distinction is fundamental.

A patient may knowingly accept a recognised risk of a properly performed procedure. That does not mean the patient has agreed that the practitioner may perform the procedure negligently.

For example, a patient may be warned that a procedure carries a recognised risk of nerve injury. If the nerve injury occurs despite reasonable treatment, the disclosed risk may be highly relevant to the claim.

But if the nerve injury resulted from negligent performance of the procedure, the mere fact that nerve injury was listed on a consent form does not automatically excuse the negligent conduct.

Core distinction: Consent to a known risk is not the same thing as consent to negligent treatment.

8. The Practitioner May Still Be Liable Despite Consent

A patient agreeing to treatment does not provide a general indemnity against every consequence of the practitioner's conduct.

There may be separate legal questions concerning:

  1. whether the patient consented to the treatment;
  2. whether the consent was properly informed;
  3. whether the practitioner disclosed material risks;
  4. whether the treatment was performed with reasonable skill and care;
  5. whether the practitioner was negligent;
  6. whether the negligent conduct caused the claimed harm; and
  7. whether the informed-consent issue itself caused legally relevant harm.

These questions should not be collapsed into one enquiry.

9. Informed Consent and Negligence Are Not Always the Same Claim

A failure to obtain informed consent and negligent performance of treatment may arise from the same clinical episode but involve different legal enquiries.

The practitioner may have performed the procedure according to an appropriate professional standard but failed to disclose a material risk.

Alternatively, the practitioner may have properly disclosed the risks but performed the procedure negligently.

It is also possible for both issues to arise in the same case.

S v Maitin

The Supreme Court of Appeal discussed the relationship between informed consent and negligence and emphasised that the questions should not simply be treated as interchangeable.

10. The Importance of the Patient's Particular Circumstances

The disclosure enquiry is not necessarily satisfied by giving every patient the same standardised list of risks.

The practitioner's duty may be affected by the patient's particular circumstances and by information known to the practitioner about what matters would be significant to that patient.

This is particularly important where the patient's personal circumstances, occupation, lifestyle, future plans or particular concerns make a particular risk especially significant.

The investigation should therefore ask not only:

"Was this risk generally disclosed?"

but also:

"Was this risk significant for this patient in these circumstances?"

11. Alternatives Matter

Informed decision-making may require more than disclosure of the risks of the recommended procedure.

The patient may also need information concerning reasonably available alternatives and the consequences of refusing treatment, depending on the circumstances.

Section 6 of the National Health Act expressly refers to the range of diagnostic procedures and treatment options generally available, as well as the benefits, risks, costs and consequences generally associated with each option.

A consent investigation should therefore identify what options were reasonably available at the time and whether the patient was given sufficient information to make the decision.

12. Emergency Situations

The ordinary requirement for informed consent is subject to statutory exceptions.

Section 7 of the National Health Act recognises circumstances in which informed consent cannot ordinarily be obtained, including specified situations involving incapacity, authorised consent, serious risks to public health, and circumstances in which delay may result in death or irreversible harm.

An emergency should therefore be investigated in its actual context. The absence of a conventional signed consent form does not necessarily establish unlawful treatment where a recognised exception applies.

13. The Consent Form Investigation

When reviewing a medical record, the consent documentation should be examined alongside the clinical chronology.

Consent Audit

  • Is there a consent form?
  • What procedure does the form actually describe?
  • Was the form signed before the procedure?
  • Who obtained the consent?
  • What risks are expressly recorded?
  • Are serious or material risks identified?
  • Are alternatives recorded where relevant?
  • Does the clinical record contain evidence of a discussion?
  • Was the patient given an opportunity to ask questions?
  • Was the patient's understanding assessed?
  • Was the patient competent to consent at the relevant time?
  • Was consent voluntary?
  • Was the procedure ultimately performed the procedure for which consent was obtained?

14. The Risk That Materialised

When the harm suffered by the patient is itself a recognised risk of treatment, the practitioner should not immediately assume that the claim fails.

Instead, separate the questions:

  1. Was the risk inherent in the procedure?
  2. Was the risk material?
  3. Was it disclosed?
  4. Did the patient understand and accept it?
  5. Was the procedure nevertheless performed negligently?
  6. Would the patient have made a different decision if properly informed?
  7. What harm actually resulted?

These questions prevent the investigation from treating "known risk" as synonymous with "no liability".

15. The Patient Who Says, "I Would Never Have Agreed"

In a claim based specifically on failure to disclose a material risk, causation can become an important issue.

The investigation may need to establish what the patient would have done if the relevant information had been provided.

The question is not merely whether the practitioner should have disclosed the risk. The legal significance of the omission may also depend on whether proper disclosure would have changed the patient's decision and whether that connects the omission to the harm claimed.

Somke v Ostrofsky

The court considered the relationship between failure to disclose a risk, the patient's decision, and causation in a claim involving informed consent.

16. A Critical Distinction for the Practitioner

The most important analytical distinction in this chapter can be expressed simply:

"The patient accepted the risk" is not the same proposition as "the practitioner was entitled to cause the injury in any manner."

If the injury was a recognised consequence of properly performed treatment and the patient was properly informed of the material risk, that fact may be highly significant.

If the injury resulted from negligent treatment, disclosure of the risk does not automatically convert negligent conduct into reasonable treatment.

17. The Investigative Questions

Before Advising the Client

  • What exactly did the patient consent to?
  • What information was provided before consent?
  • What risks were disclosed?
  • Which risk actually materialised?
  • Was that risk material?
  • Was the patient capable of understanding and deciding?
  • Was the information communicated in a manner the patient could understand?
  • Were reasonable alternatives discussed?
  • Was the patient's right to refuse respected?
  • Was there an emergency or statutory exception?
  • Was the treatment itself performed negligently?
  • Would the patient have refused or chosen differently if properly informed?
  • What evidence proves the answer to each question?

18. The Practitioner’s Preliminary Test

At the end of the consent investigation, the practitioner should be able to separate the case into distinct propositions.

Consent: Did the patient agree to the proposed treatment?

Information: Was the patient given the information required to make an informed decision?

Risk: Was the material risk disclosed and understood?

Performance: Was the treatment itself performed with reasonable skill and care?

Causation: What legal significance does the failure, if any, have for the harm claimed?
