The Standard of Care

Once the facts have been tested, the next question is not simply whether something went wrong. The question is whether the practitioner failed to exercise the degree of professional skill and care that the law required in the circumstances.

Practitioner principle: A poor outcome does not, by itself, establish negligent medical treatment. The investigation must identify what should reasonably have been done, what was actually done, and whether the difference amounts to negligence.

1. Negligence Is Judged Against a Standard

South African negligence law asks whether a reasonable person in the position of the defendant would have foreseen the reasonable possibility of harm and would have taken reasonable steps to guard against that harm, and whether the defendant failed to take those steps.

In a medical-negligence case, however, the enquiry must take account of the professional nature of the conduct being examined. The standard is therefore informed by the skill and care reasonably expected from a practitioner in the relevant branch of the profession.

The familiar general negligence test is associated with Kruger v Coetzee.

Kruger v Coetzee

The case provides the general South African framework for determining negligence: reasonable foreseeability, reasonable precautions, and failure to take those precautions.

Read Kruger v Coetzee on SAFLII →

2. The Medical Practitioner Is Not Required to Be Perfect

Medical practice does not impose a requirement of perfection. The practitioner is not expected to possess or exercise the highest possible degree of professional skill in every circumstance.

The classic formulation comes from Mitchell v Dixon, and was subsequently applied and explained in Van Wyk v Lewis.

The enquiry is whether the practitioner employed reasonable skill and care, having regard to the circumstances and the professional field in which the practitioner was operating.

Mitchell v Dixon

The Supreme Court of Appeal has repeatedly relied on the principle from this case that a medical practitioner is not expected to bring the highest possible degree of professional skill to every case, but is required to employ reasonable skill and care.

Read the discussion of Mitchell v Dixon in Medi-Clinic v Vermeulen on SAFLII →

3. The Relevant Professional Standard

Van Wyk v Lewis remains an important South African authority on the professional standard applicable to medical practitioners.

The court considers the general level of skill and diligence possessed and exercised at the relevant time by members of the branch of the profession to which the practitioner belongs.

This means that the standard cannot safely be described in the abstract. The practitioner, the field of practice, the patient's circumstances, the available information, and the circumstances in which treatment was provided all matter.

Van Wyk v Lewis

The case concerned the standard of professional skill and care expected of a medical practitioner and remains a foundational authority in South African medical-negligence law.

Read the discussion of Van Wyk v Lewis on SAFLII →

4. The Circumstances Matter

The standard of care cannot be separated from the circumstances in which the practitioner had to make the decision.

The practitioner may have been dealing with an emergency, incomplete information, limited resources, an unusual presentation, competing clinical risks, or a rapidly deteriorating patient.

The investigation should therefore reconstruct the circumstances as they existed when the decision was made.

Avoid hindsight. The question is not simply whether the eventual outcome makes the decision look wrong. The question is what a reasonable practitioner could and should have done on the information reasonably available at the relevant time.

5. An Unfavourable Outcome Is Not the Standard

One of the most important distinctions in medical-negligence investigation is the difference between an adverse outcome and negligent conduct.

A treatment can be properly performed and still produce an adverse result. A diagnosis can be reasonable even though another diagnosis would ultimately have been correct. A recognised treatment can fail. A complication can occur despite appropriate precautions.

The investigator must therefore resist reasoning backwards from the injury.

The fact that the patient suffered harm creates the need for investigation. It does not itself answer the negligence question.

6. Clinical Judgment Does Not Automatically Equal Negligence

Medical treatment frequently involves professional judgment. Different competent practitioners may sometimes select different reasonable approaches to the same clinical problem.

The existence of more than one possible approach does not automatically mean that one of those approaches was negligent.

The legal investigation must instead determine whether the treatment or decision fell outside the range of reasonable professional conduct in the circumstances.

This is particularly important where experts disagree. A legal practitioner should not simply select the expert whose opinion produces the desired outcome. The reasoning supporting each opinion must be examined.

7. Expert Evidence and the Standard of Care

Expert evidence is often central to establishing the professional standard. The court, however, does not simply outsource the legal decision to an expert.

In Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another, the Supreme Court of Appeal dealt extensively with the evaluation of conflicting expert medical evidence.

Where competing medical opinions exist, the court must evaluate the reasoning behind those opinions and determine which evidence is persuasive and logically supported.

Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic

This is a key authority for the investigation of expert evidence in medical-negligence litigation. The court's task is not merely to count experts on each side, but to evaluate the reasoning and factual foundation underlying their opinions.

Read Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic on SAFLII →

8. Guidelines, Protocols and Hospital Policies

Clinical guidelines, hospital protocols, policies, standard operating procedures and professional guidance can be important evidence when determining what should reasonably have been done.

They should nevertheless be investigated carefully.

A departure from a guideline does not automatically establish negligence. Equally, compliance with a written protocol does not necessarily end the enquiry if the circumstances required a different reasonable response.

The important question is what the guideline, protocol or policy shows about reasonable practice in the particular circumstances, and whether the practitioner had a justified reason for departing from it.

9. The Standard Must Be Defined Precisely

A weak medical-negligence case often contains a vague proposition such as:

"The doctor should have treated the patient better."

That is not yet a useful standard-of-care proposition.

The practitioner should instead formulate the proposition with sufficient precision to identify the alleged departure.

Useful formulation: At the relevant time, given the patient's condition, the information available and the circumstances confronting the practitioner, a reasonable practitioner in the relevant field would have taken specific step X. The defendant instead took step Y or failed to take X.

10. Build the Standard-of-Care Proposition

Before obtaining an expert opinion, the legal practitioner should attempt to identify the precise clinical proposition that requires testing.

Standard-of-Care Questions

  • What was the patient's condition at the relevant time?
  • What information was available to the practitioner?
  • What clinical decision was made?
  • What alternative decision is alleged to have been required?
  • What risk was reasonably foreseeable?
  • What reasonable precaution is said to have been required?
  • Was the practitioner working in an emergency or routine setting?
  • What resources and information were actually available?
  • What professional field or speciality is relevant?
  • What evidence supports the proposed standard?
  • Does the expert's opinion explain why the proposed standard was required?

11. Separate the Standard From Causation

A practitioner may have departed from the appropriate standard without that departure causing the injury claimed by the patient.

Conversely, an injury may have occurred because of a negligent act, but the alleged standard must still be established.

The legal investigation should therefore keep two questions separate:

  1. Was the practitioner's conduct negligent?
  2. Did that negligent conduct cause the legally relevant harm?

Combining these questions too early can produce a retrospective argument in which the severity of the injury is used to prove that the treatment must have been negligent.

12. The Practitioner Who Was Present Is Not Necessarily the Person Who Breached the Standard

In a hospital environment, several people may contribute to the care of the same patient.

The investigator should identify the specific decision, omission or act attributed to each defendant.

For example, an alleged failure to monitor may involve different responsibilities for the attending doctor, nursing staff, anaesthetist, specialist, hospital or other healthcare professional.

The standard must therefore be matched to the role actually performed.

13. A Practical Medical-Negligence Audit

At the end of this stage, the file should contain more than a general allegation of poor treatment. It should contain a testable proposition.

The Audit

  • Patient: What was the patient's clinical condition?
  • Time: At what precise point did the alleged breach occur?
  • Information: What did the practitioner know then?
  • Decision: What did the practitioner actually do?
  • Standard: What should reasonably have been done?
  • Evidence: What supports that proposed standard?
  • Departure: What exactly was different?
  • Risk: What harm was reasonably foreseeable?
  • Causation: How is the alleged departure connected to the injury?
  • Alternative explanation: What other explanation exists for the outcome?

The Preliminary Test

A viable medical-negligence investigation should eventually be capable of stating the case in one clear proposition:

This was the patient's condition; this was what the practitioner knew; this was the decision made; this was what reasonable professional practice required; this is the evidence establishing that standard; this is the departure; and this is how the departure caused the harm.

If the investigation cannot yet make that statement, the case may still be viable. But the missing component should be identified before the matter is treated as ready for litigation.
