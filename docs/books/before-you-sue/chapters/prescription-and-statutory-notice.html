Prescription Is an Investigation Issue

Prescription should be investigated at the beginning of a medical-negligence matter, not treated as a procedural issue to be considered shortly before summons is issued.

A potentially strong claim may be lost if the debt has prescribed. Conversely, a claim should not automatically be treated as prescribed merely because three years have passed since the treatment or operation.

Practitioner principle: Build the prescription timeline from the legally relevant dates and facts. Do not simply count three years from the date of the medical procedure.

The Prescription Act 68 of 1969

The starting point is the Prescription Act 68 of 1969. Section 11(d) generally provides a three-year prescription period for "any other debt", unless another Act provides otherwise.

A medical-negligence damages claim will ordinarily fall within the three-year period contemplated by section 11(d).

But the important investigative question is not simply: When did the treatment occur?

The more important questions are:

  • When did the debt become due?
  • When did the patient know the identity of the potential debtor?
  • When did the patient know the material facts from which the debt arose?
  • Could that knowledge reasonably have been acquired earlier?
  • Was prescription delayed or interrupted?
  • Was a claim made against an organ of state?
  • If so, was the statutory notice requirement complied with?

The official text of the Prescription Act provides that prescription ordinarily begins when the debt is due, while section 12(3) deals specifically with knowledge of the debtor's identity and the facts from which the debt arises.

Section 12: When Does Prescription Begin?

Section 12(1) provides the general rule: prescription commences when the debt is due.

Section 12(3), however, provides an important qualification. A debt is not regarded as due until the creditor has knowledge of the identity of the debtor and of the facts from which the debt arises.

The qualification is subject to the reasonable-care proviso. A creditor is deemed to have the relevant knowledge if it could have been acquired by exercising reasonable care.

This means that the practitioner investigating prescription must establish the actual factual history rather than selecting an arbitrary starting date.

Truter v Deysel: Knowledge of Facts, Not the Legal Conclusion

The leading medical-negligence authority is Truter and Another v Deysel.

The case concerned the meaning of "knowledge of the facts from which the debt arises" in section 12(3) of the Prescription Act in the context of a medical negligence claim.

The important distinction is between knowledge of the material facts and knowledge of the legal conclusion that those facts amount to negligence.

A claimant does not necessarily have to know that a practitioner was legally negligent before prescription can begin to run.

The investigation therefore asks whether the claimant knew the material facts from which the elements of the claim could be established.

Do Not Wait for Certainty

A common investigative mistake is to assume that prescription only begins once the claimant has obtained an expert opinion confirming negligence.

That is too broad a proposition.

The law does not generally require the claimant to possess every piece of evidence necessary to prove the case before prescription can begin.

The relevant enquiry concerns the material facts from which the debt arises. The fact that a claimant still needs expert evidence to determine whether those facts establish negligence does not automatically postpone prescription.

Investigative warning: "I did not yet know that the practitioner was negligent" is not the same proposition as "I did not yet know the material facts from which my claim arose."

Truter and the Medical Timeline

In a medical-negligence investigation, the practitioner should construct a knowledge timeline.

  1. Date of the medical treatment or procedure.
  2. Date of the adverse event or complication.
  3. Date on which the patient first became aware of the injury.
  4. Date on which the patient became aware of the relevant treatment history.
  5. Date on which the patient knew, or could reasonably have known, the identity of the potential debtor.
  6. Date on which the patient knew the material facts from which the debt arose.
  7. Date on which prescription consequently began to run.
  8. Any event that delayed, interrupted or affected prescription.

The chronology should be supported by the medical record, correspondence, referrals, complaints, consultations, expert reports and the patient's own account.

Prescription Is Not the Same as the Merits

Prescription and negligence answer different questions.

  • Negligence: Was the defendant legally at fault?
  • Causation: Did the conduct cause the legally recoverable harm?
  • Damage: What loss resulted?
  • Prescription: Has the debt become extinguished by the passage of time?

A claim may have strong merits but still be prescribed. Conversely, a claim may be within time but fail because negligence, causation or damage cannot be established.

The practitioner should therefore run a prescription investigation alongside the merits investigation rather than treating prescription as an afterthought.

Interruption and Delay

The prescription calculation should not stop once a preliminary starting date has been identified.

The Prescription Act contains provisions dealing with circumstances that may delay the completion of prescription and circumstances that may interrupt it.

Section 13: Delayed Completion

Section 13 deals with circumstances in which prescription may not complete despite the ordinary period having run, including specified relationships or impediments.

The practitioner should therefore check the facts against section 13 rather than assuming that the ordinary three-year period operates without exception.

Section 14: Acknowledgement of Liability

Section 14 deals with interruption of prescription by acknowledgement of liability.

Correspondence from a healthcare institution, insurer or other potential debtor should therefore be preserved and reviewed carefully. The legal effect of particular correspondence must be determined from its wording and context.

Section 15: Judicial Interruption

Section 15 deals with interruption by service of process whereby the creditor claims payment of the debt.

Practical rule: Never calculate a prescription deadline from one date alone. Build a timeline that records possible interruption, acknowledgement and statutory events.

The Constitutional Court and Prescription

The Constitutional Court has considered the distinction between knowledge of the facts giving rise to a debt and knowledge that those facts constitute a legally actionable wrong.

Mtokonya v Minister of Police

Mtokonya v Minister of Police [2017] ZACC 33; 2018 (5) SA 22 (CC).

The judgment provides important guidance on the interpretation of section 12(3), including the distinction between knowledge of the facts and knowledge of their legal consequences.

For medical-negligence practitioners, the lesson is important: the prescription enquiry should identify the material factual knowledge possessed by the claimant, rather than simply asking when the claimant first obtained a legal opinion.

Claims Against the State: The Additional Notice Requirement

A medical-negligence claim against a provincial health department or another qualifying organ of state requires an additional procedural investigation.

The Institution of Legal Proceedings Against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 regulates notice before proceedings for the recovery of a debt may be instituted against an organ of state.

This statutory notice requirement must be kept conceptually separate from prescription.

Two separate clocks: Prescription concerns the extinction of the debt through the operation of prescription law. The statutory notice requirement concerns the procedural steps required before proceedings may be instituted against a qualifying organ of state.

Section 3 Notice

Section 3(1) provides that legal proceedings for the recovery of a debt may not be instituted against an organ of state unless the required written notice has been given, or the organ of state has consented in writing to proceedings without compliant notice.

Under section 3(2), the notice must generally be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due.

The notice must briefly set out:

  • The facts giving rise to the debt.
  • The particulars of the debt that are within the creditor's knowledge.

Section 3(3) also contains a knowledge rule. For purposes of the notice period, the debt is not regarded as due until the creditor has knowledge of the identity of the organ of state and the facts giving rise to the debt, subject to the reasonable-care proviso.

Do Not Confuse Six Months With Three Years

One of the most dangerous errors in a state medical-negligence claim is to treat the six-month statutory notice period as though it were the prescription period.

They are different legal requirements.

Issue Prescription Section 3 Notice
Primary legislation Prescription Act 68 of 1969 Act 40 of 2002
General period Three years for an ordinary "other debt" Six months from when the debt became due
Function Governs extinction of the debt by prescription Pre-proceedings notice requirement against qualifying organs of state
Can they overlap? Yes. Both must be investigated independently.

Condonation of Late State Notice

Failure to give the required notice within the statutory period does not mean that every claim is automatically beyond recovery.

Section 3(4) permits a creditor to apply for condonation where an organ of state relies on the failure to give notice.

The court may grant condonation where the statutory requirements are satisfied, including that:

  1. The debt has not been extinguished by prescription.
  2. Good cause exists for the failure.
  3. The organ of state was not unreasonably prejudiced by the failure.

The first requirement is particularly important. Condonation of a late statutory notice is not a mechanism for reviving a debt that has already prescribed.

What Must Be Investigated in a State Hospital Claim?

When the potential defendant is a provincial health department or another qualifying organ of state, the initial investigation should establish:

  • Exactly which entity employed or operated the relevant healthcare facility.
  • Whether the defendant is an organ of state contemplated by Act 40 of 2002.
  • The date on which the debt became due.
  • The date on which the claimant knew, or could reasonably have known, the relevant identity and facts.
  • The date by which section 3 notice had to be served.
  • Whether notice was actually served.
  • How notice was served and on whom.
  • Whether the notice contained the required particulars.
  • Whether any written consent to non-compliance was obtained.
  • Whether proceedings were instituted after the applicable notice period.
  • Whether prescription has been interrupted or delayed.
  • Whether condonation is required.

The Prescription Audit

Before deciding that a medical-negligence matter is viable, complete a prescription audit.

Prescription audit:

  • ☐ Date of treatment identified.
  • ☐ Date of injury or adverse outcome identified.
  • ☐ Potential debtor identified.
  • ☐ Material facts giving rise to the debt identified.
  • ☐ Date of actual knowledge investigated.
  • ☐ Reasonable-care issue investigated.
  • ☐ Prescription commencement date calculated.
  • ☐ Three-year period calculated where applicable.
  • ☐ Section 13 issues checked.
  • ☐ Acknowledgement of liability under section 14 checked.
  • ☐ Judicial interruption under section 15 checked.
  • ☐ State defendant identified, if applicable.
  • ☐ Six-month section 3 notice deadline calculated, if applicable.
  • ☐ Notice content and service checked.
  • ☐ Condonation issue identified, if necessary.

The Go / No-Go Question

At the end of this chapter, the practitioner should be able to answer a much more precise question than: "Is the claim prescribed?"

The proper question is:

What is the legally relevant date on which the debt became due, what events affected the running or completion of prescription, and, if the defendant is an organ of state, was the statutory notice requirement complied with?

Only once those questions have been answered should the investigation move confidently toward the final viability decision.
