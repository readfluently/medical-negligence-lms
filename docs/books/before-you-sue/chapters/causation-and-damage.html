Causation and Damage

Establishing negligence is not the end of a medical-negligence claim. The practitioner may have acted negligently, but the claimant must still establish that the wrongful and negligent conduct caused the harm for which compensation is sought.

Practitioner principle: Do not reason backwards from the patient's injury. Identify the alleged breach, reconstruct what probably would have happened if reasonable care had been provided, and then determine whether the claimed harm probably resulted from the breach.

1. Negligence Does Not Automatically Establish Liability

A finding that a practitioner failed to meet the required standard of care does not, by itself, establish an entitlement to damages.

The claimant must establish a causal connection between the wrongful and negligent conduct and the harm suffered.

This distinction is particularly important in medicine because patients may already have serious diseases, underlying conditions, congenital abnormalities, unavoidable complications, or injuries that would have occurred even if reasonable treatment had been provided.

2. Two Questions of Causation

South African law generally separates causation into two enquiries:

  1. Factual causation — did the wrongful conduct actually cause the loss?
  2. Legal causation — is the connection between the conduct and the loss sufficiently close for legal liability to follow?

These are related but distinct enquiries.

International Shipping Co (Pty) Ltd v Bentley

The Supreme Court of Appeal formulated the classic distinction between factual causation and legal causation. Factual causation ordinarily involves the "but-for" enquiry, while legal causation asks whether the wrongful conduct is sufficiently closely connected to the loss for liability to follow.

Read International Shipping Co v Bentley on SAFLII →

3. Factual Causation: The But-For Enquiry

The factual causation enquiry asks a hypothetical question:

If the negligent conduct is mentally removed and reasonable conduct is substituted, would the harm probably still have occurred?

If the harm would probably have occurred anyway, the negligent conduct may not be the factual cause of that harm.

If the harm probably would not have occurred, the negligent conduct may satisfy factual causation.

This is commonly referred to as the but-for test.

4. The But-For Test Is Not a Mathematical Formula

Medical causation is rarely capable of absolute scientific certainty.

The enquiry is conducted on the evidence and according to the civil standard of proof. The claimant generally needs to establish that the alleged negligent conduct was probably a cause of the harm.

Courts have described the enquiry as a practical, common-sense assessment rather than an exercise requiring metaphysical or absolute certainty.

Chapeikin and Another v Mini

The Supreme Court of Appeal reiterated that the but-for enquiry requires a practical assessment of what probably would have happened absent the wrongful and negligent conduct.

Read Chapeikin v Mini on SAFLII →

5. Causation in an Omission Case

Medical-negligence claims frequently concern an omission: a practitioner failed to diagnose, failed to monitor, failed to refer, failed to investigate, failed to treat, failed to intervene, or failed to take another precaution.

In such a case, the investigator must identify the reasonable step that should have been taken and then construct the appropriate hypothetical.

The question becomes:

What would probably have happened if the practitioner had taken the reasonable step when it should have been taken?

The answer cannot simply be assumed. It must be supported by the clinical evidence, chronology and, where appropriate, expert evidence.

6. Timing Can Be the Causal Issue

In medical negligence, the alleged breach may not itself produce the injury immediately.

Instead, the allegation may be that an omission caused a delay, and that the delay reduced the patient's opportunity for successful treatment.

Examples include:

  • failure to diagnose a serious condition;
  • failure to obtain an appropriate investigation;
  • failure to recognise deterioration;
  • failure to refer to a specialist;
  • failure to perform an emergency intervention; or
  • failure to commence treatment within an appropriate period.

In these cases, the critical question may be what difference earlier or appropriate intervention would probably have made.

7. Oppelt: The Importance of the Hypothetical Course of Events

The Head: Health, Department of Health, Provincial Administration: Western Cape v Oppelt is an important authority for understanding factual causation in a medical setting.

The case concerned a serious spinal injury and the allegation that delayed medical intervention caused or contributed to the subsequent paralysis.

The Supreme Court of Appeal emphasised the factual causation enquiry and ultimately found, on the evidence, that the treatment complained of was not a factual cause of the paralysis.

Oppelt

The case is particularly useful because it demonstrates that the court must investigate what probably would have happened if the alleged negligent conduct had not occurred, rather than assuming causation from the seriousness of the eventual injury.

Read The Head: Health v Oppelt on SAFLII →

8. The "What Would Have Happened?" Question

A useful medical-negligence investigation should therefore create two timelines.

Actual Timeline

  • What happened to the patient?
  • When did symptoms appear?
  • When was the patient assessed?
  • What decisions were made?
  • What treatment was given?
  • When did the condition deteriorate?
  • What injury ultimately occurred?

Hypothetical Reasonable-Care Timeline

  • When should the condition have been recognised?
  • What investigation should have been performed?
  • What treatment should have followed?
  • When should that treatment have occurred?
  • What would probably have happened after that intervention?
  • What injury, if any, would probably still have occurred?

9. The Difference Between Possibility and Probability

One of the most common weaknesses in medical-negligence cases is confusing a possible causal explanation with a probable one.

An expert may be able to say that earlier treatment could have changed the outcome. That does not necessarily establish that earlier treatment probably would have changed the outcome.

The legal practitioner should therefore interrogate the language used in expert reports.

Watch the distinction:

"It is possible that earlier treatment would have prevented the injury."

is not automatically equivalent to:

"It is more probable than not that earlier treatment would have prevented the injury."

10. Expert Evidence and Causation

Expert evidence may be essential where the causal question involves specialised medical knowledge.

But the expert must identify the factual foundation for the opinion. A causal opinion should not simply assume that the alleged negligence caused the injury because the injury followed the treatment.

The expert should explain the mechanism of injury, the relevant chronology, the patient's pre-existing condition, alternative causes, and the probable effect of the proposed reasonable intervention.

Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic

Expert opinion must be examined for logical reasoning and factual foundation. This is particularly important where competing experts offer different views on causation.

Read Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic on SAFLII →

11. Multiple Causes

A patient's injury may have more than one cause.

For example, the final outcome may have been influenced by:

  • the patient's underlying disease;
  • a pre-existing injury;
  • the natural progression of the condition;
  • the negligent act or omission;
  • another medical condition;
  • an unavoidable complication; or
  • a later independent event.

The existence of another possible cause does not automatically defeat the claim. It does, however, make careful causal analysis essential.

12. Pre-Existing Conditions

A claimant does not necessarily fail simply because the patient had a pre-existing condition.

The question is what harm was caused or materially contributed to by the defendant's wrongful conduct.

The practitioner should therefore establish the patient's condition before the alleged breach as accurately as possible.

Investigative rule: Always establish the patient's baseline condition before attempting to quantify the harm allegedly caused by negligent treatment.

13. Aggravation of an Existing Condition

The alleged negligence may not have created the entire medical problem. It may instead have aggravated an existing condition or caused an additional injury.

The causal question should then be framed around the difference between the patient's actual condition and the condition the patient probably would have had absent the negligent conduct.

This distinction can become important when assessing both liability and damages.

14. Legal Causation

Even where factual causation is established, the enquiry does not necessarily end.

The law must still determine whether the connection between the wrongful conduct and the loss is sufficiently close or direct for liability to follow.

This is commonly referred to as legal causation, or the question of remoteness.

It is a juridical enquiry in which considerations concerning the limits of liability and legal policy may become relevant.

15. Do Not Confuse Causation With Quantum

Causation and damages are connected, but they are not identical enquiries.

First establish what injury or loss was caused by the wrongful conduct. Only then should the practitioner move to the question of how that harm should be translated into monetary damages.

The investigation should therefore distinguish:

  1. the patient's condition before the breach;
  2. the injury or deterioration caused by the breach;
  3. the consequences flowing from that injury; and
  4. the financial value of those consequences.

16. Categories of Damage

Depending on the facts, a medical-negligence claim may involve different forms of loss and damage.

  • past medical and hospital expenses;
  • future medical and related care;
  • loss of earnings or earning capacity;
  • the cost of assistance or care;
  • physical pain and suffering;
  • psychological or emotional consequences;
  • loss of amenities of life; and
  • other legally recognised consequences of the injury.

The precise heads of damage depend on the facts and the applicable principles governing the claim.

17. The Damage Must Be Linked to the Injury

It is not sufficient to identify every financial or personal difficulty experienced by the claimant after the medical event.

The practitioner must establish which consequences actually flow from the injury caused by the defendant's conduct.

For example, if the alleged negligence caused a permanent physical impairment, the investigation should identify the consequences of that impairment rather than simply listing every expense incurred after the medical treatment.

18. The Chain of Consequences

A useful way to investigate damages is to construct a chain:

Alleged breach → clinical consequence → injury → functional consequence → financial or personal loss

Every significant link should be supported by evidence.

Medical evidence may establish the injury and functional consequence. Employment evidence may establish loss of earnings. Financial records may establish expenditure. Expert evidence may be required to establish future care or earning capacity.

19. The Evidence Matrix

Causation Evidence

  • What was the alleged negligent act or omission?
  • What injury is alleged to have resulted?
  • What is the patient's baseline condition?
  • What alternative causes exist?
  • What would reasonable treatment probably have achieved?
  • What evidence supports the counterfactual?
  • Which expert addresses causation?
  • Are the expert's assumptions supported by the record?
  • Is the causal opinion expressed in terms of probability?
  • What damages are directly linked to the proven injury?

20. The Practitioner Must Resist the "Outcome Equals Cause" Fallacy

A serious injury can make negligence appear obvious after the event. This is dangerous reasoning.

The investigator should instead ask:

What evidence demonstrates that the injury was caused by the defendant's breach rather than by the underlying disease, unavoidable progression, recognised complication or another cause?

This question should be answered before the value of the claim becomes the focus of the investigation.

21. The Burden of Proof

In a civil medical-negligence claim, the claimant generally bears the onus of proving the required elements on a balance of probabilities.

Causation therefore requires a sufficiently persuasive evidential foundation. Absolute certainty is not the standard, but speculation is not enough.

M.R obo M.A v MEC for Health: Gauteng Provincial Government

The court reiterated that a claimant is not required to establish causation with certainty, but must establish that the wrongful conduct was probably a cause of the loss through a sensible retrospective analysis based on the evidence.

Read M.R obo M.A v MEC for Health on SAFLII →

22. A Recent Illustration: K.E.M obo O.M

Recent South African medical-negligence litigation continues to apply the same basic framework: the claimant must establish factual causation on a balance of probabilities, while expert evidence must be evaluated critically rather than accepted merely because it is professionally expressed.

K.E.M obo O.M v MEC for Health, North West Provincial Government

The 2026 judgment reiterates the but-for approach to factual causation and the need for a practical, evidence-based assessment of what would probably have happened without the negligent conduct.

Read K.E.M obo O.M on SAFLII →

23. The Causation Proposition

Before proceeding with litigation, the practitioner should be able to formulate the causal proposition clearly.

Because the practitioner failed to do X at time Y, the patient probably suffered Z, whereas with reasonable treatment the patient probably would have avoided or materially reduced Z.

The proposition should then be tested against the medical records, chronology, expert evidence and alternative explanations.

24. The Go-Further Questions

Before Advising on Viability

  • What exactly is the alleged breach?
  • What injury is said to have resulted?
  • Would the injury probably have occurred anyway?
  • What would reasonable treatment probably have achieved?
  • What is the patient's pre-existing condition?
  • Are there competing causal explanations?
  • Is the expert opinion based on the actual clinical chronology?
  • Is the causal opinion probable or merely possible?
  • Is the loss sufficiently closely connected to the wrongful conduct?
  • Which damages are actually attributable to the proven injury?

The Preliminary Test

A medical-negligence claim should not proceed merely because there is an identifiable breach and a serious injury.

The practitioner should be able to explain the causal bridge between them.

What happened, what should have happened, what probably would have happened if reasonable care had been provided, and what loss probably resulted from the difference?
