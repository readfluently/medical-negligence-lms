Testing the Facts

By this stage, the investigation should have moved beyond the initial patient story. The potential defendants have been identified and the relevant medical records have been obtained or actively pursued.

The next task is to test what is actually known.

Do not ask whether the client's version sounds convincing. Ask whether the evidence supports it.

This is the stage at which assumptions should be challenged, contradictions identified, alternative explanations considered, and unanswered questions recorded.

From Allegation to Fact

A client may say:

"The doctor failed to treat me properly."

That is an allegation.

It is not yet a fact that proves negligence.

The practitioner must unpack the allegation.

Break the Allegation Down

  • What exactly did the practitioner do?
  • What exactly did the practitioner fail to do?
  • When did the act or omission occur?
  • What information was available at that time?
  • What did the patient know?
  • What do the contemporaneous records show?
  • What evidence supports the allegation?
  • What evidence contradicts it?
  • What remains unknown?

The Four Levels of Information

A useful investigation distinguishes between four different levels of information.

1. The Patient's Account

What the patient remembers, was told, experienced, or believes happened.

2. The Documentary Record

What the clinical records, investigations, prescriptions, notes and other documents actually record.

3. Independent Evidence

Evidence from witnesses, subsequent treating practitioners, experts, photographs, communications or other sources.

4. The Legal Proposition

The conclusion the evidence is ultimately being used to establish, such as negligence, causation, wrongfulness or damage.

Never treat the fourth level as though it were already established merely because it appears in the first level.

Build a Fact Matrix

Before forming a conclusion, place the important allegations into a simple fact matrix.

Fact Matrix

  • Allegation: What is being alleged?
  • Source: Who says this happened?
  • Record: What document supports or contradicts it?
  • Date: When did it allegedly happen?
  • Witness: Who can independently confirm it?
  • Expert: Is specialist interpretation required?
  • Contradiction: Is there evidence pointing in another direction?
  • Alternative: Is there another plausible explanation?
  • Status: Proven, supported, disputed or unknown?

This prevents the investigation from becoming a collection of documents without a structured assessment of what those documents establish.

Chronology Is a Testing Tool

The chronology created in the previous chapter should now be tested against the allegation.

Suppose the allegation is that a practitioner failed to recognise a patient's deterioration.

The chronology should establish:

  • When did deterioration begin?
  • When was it first observable?
  • Who was present?
  • Who recorded it?
  • When did the practitioner receive the information?
  • What did the practitioner do?
  • What happened afterwards?
  • When was the injury diagnosed?

The allegation may change once those times are placed next to each other.

In medical negligence, timing is often part of the evidence.

Do Not Reason Backwards From the Injury

A serious injury creates a natural temptation to search backwards for something that must have caused it.

That approach can distort the investigation.

Bad outcome ≠ negligence.

The investigation must instead ask what was reasonably foreseeable at the relevant time, what reasonable precautions were available, what the practitioner knew, and what was actually done.

The South African negligence test comes from Kruger v Coetzee and has repeatedly been applied by the courts.

The Kruger v Coetzee Test

Negligence requires an enquiry into whether a reasonable person in the position of the defendant would have foreseen the reasonable possibility of harm and would have taken reasonable steps to guard against it, and whether the defendant failed to take those steps.

The enquiry is therefore not simply:

"Did the patient suffer harm?"

It is:

"What risk was reasonably foreseeable, what reasonable steps should have been taken, and were those steps taken?"

Test the Patient's Version

Testing the facts does not mean disbelieving the patient.

It means identifying which parts of the account can be independently established.

Patient Account Audit

  • Which parts are based on personal observation?
  • Which parts were told to the patient by someone else?
  • When was the account first recorded?
  • Has the account remained consistent?
  • Does the medical record support it?
  • Does another witness support it?
  • Could the patient have observed the event described?
  • Are there gaps caused by sedation, unconsciousness or memory loss?
  • Is the patient describing a medical conclusion that actually requires expert interpretation?

Test the Practitioner’s Version

The same discipline must be applied to the defendant's account.

A practitioner's explanation is evidence to be considered. It is not automatically the final answer merely because it comes from a medical professional.

Practitioner Account Audit

  • What does the practitioner say happened?
  • When was that account first given?
  • Is it consistent with the contemporaneous record?
  • Is it consistent with the nursing record?
  • Is it consistent with investigation results?
  • Is it consistent with subsequent treatment?
  • Does another practitioner support or contradict it?
  • Is the explanation medically plausible?
  • Does it address the actual allegation?

Look for Contradictions

Contradictions should be recorded rather than silently resolved.

For example:

Patient says: "The doctor was never informed."

Nursing record says: "Doctor informed at 14:10."

The correct response is not to choose whichever entry supports the preferred theory.

The practitioner must investigate:

  • Who made each entry?
  • When was it made?
  • Was the entry contemporaneous?
  • What exactly was communicated?
  • Can another record confirm the communication?
  • Was there a subsequent response?
A contradiction is an investigation question, not an inconvenience.

Test for Alternative Explanations

One of the most important disciplines in medical-negligence investigation is deliberately searching for explanations that do not support the client's preferred theory.

Alternative Explanation Questions

  • Could the injury have existed before treatment?
  • Could the underlying disease have caused the outcome?
  • Could a known complication explain the outcome?
  • Could another medical event have caused the injury?
  • Could treatment at another facility be relevant?
  • Could the injury have developed after discharge?
  • Could the alleged injury have another mechanism?
  • Is there evidence that weakens the proposed causal connection?

The purpose is not to defend the defendant.

The purpose is to discover whether the proposed case survives serious testing.

Test the Alleged Departure From the Standard

Once the factual sequence has been established as far as possible, the practitioner can begin identifying the alleged departure from reasonable professional care.

The question should be expressed precisely.

Not: "The doctor was negligent."

But:

"At approximately 14:00, given the documented clinical findings available at that time, the practitioner allegedly failed to take ________."

The second formulation identifies a proposition that can actually be tested.

Medical Negligence Requires Professional Context

The standard applied to a medical practitioner is not a requirement of perfection.

South African courts have repeatedly recognised that a medical practitioner must exercise reasonable skill and care in the circumstances.

In Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another , the Supreme Court of Appeal considered allegations of medical negligence and the proper approach to expert evidence.

Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic

The case is particularly important because it demonstrates that the court must assess the competing expert opinions rather than simply counting how many experts support each side.

The court must consider whether the reasoning underlying an expert opinion is logical and defensible.

Therefore, the fact that a medical practitioner can find another practitioner who supports the treatment does not automatically end the negligence enquiry.

The reasoning supporting the professional opinion must itself withstand scrutiny.

Expert Evidence Is Not a Substitute for Investigation

An expert should not be asked to solve an inadequately investigated factual dispute.

Before approaching an expert, the practitioner should know:

  • What happened according to the patient?
  • What happened according to the records?
  • What does the practitioner say happened?
  • What facts are common cause?
  • What facts are disputed?
  • What facts remain unknown?
  • What exact clinical proposition requires expert assistance?
The expert should be given a clinical question to answer, not a legal conclusion to approve.

Do Not Shop for an Opinion

An expert investigation should be directed by the clinical question rather than by the desired litigation outcome.

If the first expert raises an alternative explanation or identifies evidence that weakens the claim, that information must be taken seriously.

The purpose of expert evidence is to assist the court, not to manufacture certainty.

A defensible expert opinion is more valuable than an enthusiastic expert opinion.

The Court Decides Negligence

Expert evidence is important in medical-negligence litigation because medical questions often fall outside ordinary judicial knowledge.

But the ultimate legal question of negligence remains for the court.

In N[...] v MEC for the Department of Health, Gauteng Provincial Government , the court reiterated that the determination of negligence is ultimately a matter for the court, while expert evidence assists in assessing the medical issues.

The expert explains the medicine. The court determines the legal conclusion.

Test Causation Separately

Even if a departure from reasonable professional care appears possible, the investigation must still determine whether that departure caused the injury complained of.

Therefore ask two separate questions:

Question 1: Was there a departure from the required standard of care?

Question 2: Did that departure cause the harm for which compensation is claimed?

A case may fail because the evidence does not establish the first question, the second question, or both.

The Fact-Test Table

Use This Before Forming a Legal Opinion

  • Claimed fact: What is alleged?
  • Evidence: What supports it?
  • Contradiction: What points the other way?
  • Alternative: What else could explain it?
  • Expert issue: What requires medical interpretation?
  • Legal issue: What legal element does the fact relate to?
  • Status: Established / supported / disputed / unknown.

The Red Flag Test

Certain findings should cause the practitioner to slow the investigation rather than accelerate toward litigation.

  • The patient's account conflicts materially with the records.
  • The relevant clinical event was not documented clearly.
  • There are unexplained gaps in the chronology.
  • The alleged injury existed before the treatment in question.
  • Another plausible cause has emerged.
  • The proposed defendant may not have performed the relevant act.
  • The standard-of-care allegation cannot yet be stated precisely.
  • No suitably qualified expert can support the proposed medical proposition.
  • The available evidence cannot establish causation.
A red flag does not necessarily mean "no case". It means "investigate before concluding".

The Practitioner’s Preliminary Opinion

At the end of this stage, the practitioner should be able to write a preliminary opinion that distinguishes facts from assumptions.

"The evidence presently establishes..."

followed by the facts that can actually be supported.

"The evidence presently suggests..."

followed by matters that remain probable but require further investigation.

"The evidence does not presently establish..."

followed by the missing factual or expert evidence.

This produces a much more reliable preliminary assessment than a simple conclusion that the practitioner was or was not negligent.

Practitioner’s Desk

Before Moving to the Next Chapter

  • Have the client's allegations been separated into individual propositions?
  • Has each important proposition been tested against the records?
  • Have contradictions been identified?
  • Have alternative explanations been considered?
  • Has the chronology been tested rather than merely prepared?
  • Has the alleged negligent act or omission been precisely identified?
  • Is the proposed standard-of-care question capable of being answered?
  • Has causation been kept separate from negligence?
  • Have expert questions been identified without dictating the answer?
  • Are the remaining uncertainties clearly recorded?

The Go-Further Question

Once the facts have been tested, the investigation can move to the central clinical question:

What standard of care was required in these circumstances, and was it breached?
