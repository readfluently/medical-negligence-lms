Identifying the Potential Defendant

A patient may remember the name of the doctor who treated them. That does not necessarily mean that the doctor is the only person who may be legally responsible for the harm.

The practitioner must therefore ask a broader question:

Who is legally responsible for the treatment, the healthcare environment, the conduct complained of, or the loss alleged?

This question should be answered before litigation is commenced. The person who performed the procedure, the institution where it occurred, the person's employer, and the legal entity responsible for the institution may be different persons.

The First Distinction: Who Treated the Patient?

Begin by identifying every healthcare professional involved in the relevant episode of care.

Identify the People

  • Doctor or specialist
  • General practitioner
  • Nurse or midwife
  • Surgeon or anaesthetist
  • Radiologist or pathologist
  • Allied health professional
  • Locum or sessional practitioner
  • Any other person who made or implemented a relevant clinical decision

But identification is only the beginning.

The next question is:

In what legal capacity was each person acting?

Was the practitioner an independent practitioner, an employee, a partner, a director, a contractor, a locum, or an employee of a hospital or healthcare group?

The Practitioner Is Not Always the Only Defendant

Medical negligence litigation can involve more than one potentially responsible party. A clinical event may involve the conduct of an individual practitioner as well as the organisation through which healthcare services were provided.

The investigation should therefore establish:

  • Who employed the practitioner?
  • Who owned or operated the practice?
  • Who operated the hospital or clinic?
  • Who supplied the relevant service?
  • Who controlled the relevant clinical or administrative system?
  • Was the practitioner working independently or as part of an organisation?
  • Was the practitioner acting within the scope of employment or another relationship?
  • Were other healthcare professionals involved?

These questions help prevent a common investigative error: treating the person visible in the clinical story as automatically identical to the person who should ultimately be sued.

Vicarious Liability

South African law recognises circumstances in which one person or entity may be held liable for the wrongful conduct of another, particularly where an employer is held vicariously liable for the wrongful acts or omissions of an employee committed in the course and scope of employment.

The Constitutional Court explained the general principle in F v Minister of Safety and Security and Another .

F v Minister of Safety and Security and Another

The Court explained that vicarious liability can arise where a particular relationship, such as employment, exists between the parties. As a general rule, an employer may be vicariously liable for wrongful acts or omissions committed by an employee in the course and scope of employment or in activities reasonably incidental to it.

The judgment also explains that difficult cases may arise where an employee deviates from the employer's business. In those cases the court considers whether there is a sufficiently close link between the employee's conduct and the employer's business.

The case is not a medical-negligence judgment, but its explanation of vicarious liability is important when analysing whether an institution may bear responsibility for the conduct of an employee.

Public Hospitals: The Defendant May Be the MEC

Public-sector medical negligence requires particular care when identifying the defendant. The hospital itself may be the place where treatment occurred, but the legally appropriate defendant may be the relevant provincial Member of the Executive Council for Health.

This is illustrated repeatedly in South African medical-negligence litigation.

Practical rule: Do not assume that the name appearing on the hospital building is the correct legal defendant. Establish which public authority is legally responsible for the institution and its employees.

In MEC for Health, Limpopo v L W M obo D M , the claim arose from alleged negligent treatment by hospital staff at a provincial hospital. The MEC for Health, Limpopo Province, was the appellant and the judgment expressly describes the MEC as the party who would be vicariously liable for damages caused by negligent conduct of hospital staff.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

A similar structure appears in MEC for Health and Social Development, Gauteng v MM on behalf of OM , where the claim concerned alleged negligence by hospital staff and the MEC was sued as the party responsible in law for injuries caused by the negligence of staff employed at the hospital.

The State Liability Structure

The identification of the defendant in a public-sector claim is not merely a matter of choosing the institution's name. The State Liability Act provides for the relevant executive authority to be cited as the nominal defendant or respondent in proceedings against the State.

South African medical-negligence cases therefore commonly identify the relevant provincial MEC for Health as defendant where the treatment was provided by employees of a provincial health department.

This is reflected, for example, in M.R obo M.A v MEC for Health: Gauteng Provincial Government . The judgment describes the MEC as the nominal defendant and the executive authority responsible for the provincial Department of Health, with the claim alleging vicarious liability for negligent conduct by medical staff.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Research note: Defendant identification in public-sector claims must be checked against the current statutory structure and the particular institution involved. Do not rely solely on an old precedent, a hospital's trading name, or a client's description of the institution.

Private Healthcare Is Different

A private hospital, medical practice, healthcare group, or practitioner may have a different legal structure from a provincial hospital.

The practitioner should therefore identify the legal entities involved rather than simply recording the name by which the facility is commonly known.

Private Defendant Investigation

  • Full name of the practitioner
  • Professional registration details
  • Name of the practice
  • Company or close corporation, if applicable
  • Name of the hospital or healthcare group
  • Employer of each relevant healthcare professional
  • Contractual or practice relationship where relevant
  • Address for service and correct legal identity

The purpose is not to add defendants indiscriminately. It is to establish the legal relationships before deciding who should be joined.

Do Not Confuse the Hospital With Every Person Working There

A hospital may contain doctors who are employees of the hospital, independent practitioners, specialists with admitting privileges, anaesthetists working through separate practices, nurses employed by the institution, and other service providers.

The fact that treatment occurred inside a particular hospital therefore does not, by itself, establish the legal relationship between every person involved and the hospital.

Location is evidence of where treatment occurred. It is not, by itself, proof of who employed every person involved.

The investigation should establish the relationship for the particular act or omission being challenged.

One Clinical Event Can Produce Several Potential Defendants

Consider a surgical complication.

The alleged problem may involve the surgeon's decision, the anaesthetist's management, nursing observations, the hospital's response to deterioration, or a failure in a system of care.

The correct investigation is therefore not:

"Which doctor caused the injury?"

It is:

"Which acts or omissions are alleged to have caused the injury, who performed them, and in what legal relationship were those persons acting?"

Only after answering those questions should the practitioner decide whether one defendant, several defendants, or a different legal structure needs to be considered.

Defendant Identification and Statutory Notice

Correct identification becomes particularly important where the proposed defendant is an organ of state.

The Institution of Legal Proceedings Against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 contains notice requirements concerning proceedings against certain organs of state.

Section 3 generally requires written notice of the intention to institute proceedings within the statutory period, subject to the Act's provisions concerning condonation and other requirements.

This means that identifying the correct state defendant is not merely a pleading exercise to be postponed until summons is drafted.

It belongs in the initial investigation.

Recent litigation continues to demonstrate the practical importance of these requirements. In Badenhorst and Others v MEC for Health, North West Province , the court considered an application for condonation concerning late notice in a claim arising from alleged negligent medical treatment by employees of the MEC.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner warning: Statutory notice and prescription are related but distinct issues. A practitioner should investigate both rather than assuming that compliance with one automatically satisfies the other.

The Defendant Map

Before moving into detailed medical-record analysis, create a simple defendant map.

Defendant Map

  • Person: Who personally performed the relevant act or omission?
  • Role: What was that person's professional role?
  • Status: Employee, independent practitioner, contractor, locum or other?
  • Employer: Who employed the person at the relevant time?
  • Institution: Where was the treatment provided?
  • Operator: Who legally operated the institution?
  • Public authority: If public healthcare was involved, which authority was responsible?
  • Conduct: What specific act or omission is attributed to each person?
  • Connection: What is the legal relationship between the person and the proposed defendant?
  • Time: When did the relevant conduct occur?

Do Not Add Defendants Simply to Be Safe

A preliminary investigation should be broad enough to identify the correct parties, but that does not mean every person connected with the patient's treatment should automatically be sued.

Each proposed defendant should have a reason for being included.

For every proposed defendant, ask:

What conduct, legal relationship, duty, or basis of liability connects this defendant to the claim?

This discipline prevents the defendant list from becoming a substitute for proper investigation.

The Practitioner’s Preliminary Test

Before closing the defendant-identification stage, the practitioner should be able to complete the following sentence:

"The potential claim arises from ________, which was performed or omitted by ________, who was acting as ________, for or within ________."

If the blanks cannot yet be completed, the investigation is probably not ready to move confidently into litigation.

The Go-Further Question

Once the potential defendants have been identified, the next question is whether the evidence exists to prove the relevant relationships and conduct.

That usually means obtaining the medical record, identifying the healthcare professionals from the records, and establishing who employed or engaged them.

The next investigation is not yet "Who is negligent?" The next investigation is "What do the records actually show?"

This takes the investigation naturally to the medical record.

Practitioner’s Desk

Before Moving to the Next Chapter

  • Have all healthcare professionals involved been identified?
  • Do we know what each person did?
  • Do we know when each person acted?
  • Do we know whether each person was an employee or independent practitioner?
  • Do we know who employed or engaged each relevant person?
  • Do we know who operated the relevant healthcare facility?
  • If it was a public facility, have we identified the responsible public authority?
  • Have we considered whether statutory notice requirements may apply?
  • Have we recorded the basis on which each potential defendant may be liable?
  • Have we avoided adding defendants merely because they appear somewhere in the patient's story?
