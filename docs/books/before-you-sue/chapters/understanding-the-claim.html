Understanding the Claim

Before deciding whether a medical negligence claim should be pursued, the practitioner must first understand exactly what the client is alleging.

A patient may describe a poor outcome, an unexpected complication, delayed treatment, inadequate communication, a failure to diagnose, or a belief that something went wrong. These circumstances may justify investigation, but they do not, without more, establish negligence.

Practitioner's Principle
First identify the complaint. Then identify the facts that must be established before a legal conclusion can properly be reached.

The First Task: Separate the Story From the Legal Claim

The client normally tells the story from the perspective of what happened to them. The practitioner's task is different.

The practitioner must convert the client's account into a structured factual investigation and then determine what legal issues arise from those facts.

At the outset, identify:

  • What treatment was provided?
  • Who provided the treatment?
  • When and where did the treatment occur?
  • What was the patient's condition before treatment?
  • What happened during or after treatment?
  • What does the patient say was done incorrectly?
  • What injury or deterioration is alleged?
  • What treatment followed?
  • What is the patient's current condition?
  • What loss is being claimed?

The Legal Framework

A medical negligence claim is ordinarily pursued within the law of delict. The investigation must therefore ultimately address the factual and legal requirements necessary to establish liability.

The precise formulation of the claim will depend on the facts. It is therefore dangerous to begin the investigation with a conclusion such as “the doctor was negligent” and then attempt to fit the evidence around that conclusion.

The better approach is to identify the material facts first and then determine what legal conclusions those facts may support.

Material Facts Are Not the Same as Legal Conclusions

This distinction is particularly important in medical negligence matters because the patient may know that something happened without knowing whether the healthcare practitioner acted negligently.

In Truter and Another v Deysel , the Supreme Court of Appeal considered the meaning of knowledge of “the facts from which the debt arises” in the context of a medical negligence claim.

Truter and Another v Deysel

Truter and Another v Deysel [2006] ZASCA 16; 2006 (4) SA 168 (SCA)

The case concerned prescription of a claim arising from medical treatment. The SCA explained that a debt becomes due when the creditor has a complete cause of action — in other words, when the facts necessary to establish the claim are in place.

Importantly, the Court distinguished the facts necessary to establish the claim from the evidence used to prove those facts and from the legal conclusion that negligence has occurred.

Practitioner lesson:

Do not treat “negligence” as though it were itself a primary fact that the client must necessarily know before the investigation can proceed. Identify the underlying facts first.

The decision is particularly useful when analysing the boundary between factual investigation, expert evidence, legal conclusions, and prescription.

The Claim Map

A useful preliminary map is:

Patient → Pre-treatment condition → Medical event → Treatment → Alleged problem → Injury → Subsequent treatment → Current condition → Loss

Around this sequence, identify the relevant records, witnesses, healthcare providers, potential defendants, experts, dates, and unanswered questions.

Do Not Begin With the Conclusion

Statements such as “the doctor was negligent,” “the hospital caused the injury,” or “the operation went wrong” are conclusions that still require investigation.

Replace the conclusion with a factual question:

  • What exactly did the practitioner do?
  • What did the practitioner fail to do?
  • What information was available at the time?
  • What decision was made?
  • What alternative was allegedly required?
  • What happened afterwards?
  • What evidence establishes each part of the account?
Practitioner's Principle
Do not allow a legal conclusion to replace a factual investigation.

What Was the Patient's Condition Before Treatment?

The patient's condition before the alleged negligent event is central to the investigation.

The practitioner should establish what symptoms, diagnosis, limitations, disease, or existing injury was present before the treatment in question.

This provides the baseline against which later deterioration or injury can be considered.

  • What was the original diagnosis?
  • What symptoms existed?
  • What treatment had already been provided?
  • What was the patient's functional status?
  • What was the expected course of the underlying condition?
  • Were there pre-existing conditions capable of producing the later outcome?

What Was Supposed to Happen?

The proposed treatment and the actual treatment must be distinguished.

Establish what procedure, medication, investigation, monitoring, or intervention was planned and what was actually performed.

This may require reviewing clinical records, consent documentation, theatre records, nursing notes, medication records, imaging, laboratory results, referral records, and subsequent treatment records.

What Happened Afterwards?

The investigation should then move forward in time.

Establish when the patient first noticed a problem, who was informed, what response followed, whether additional treatment was provided, and how the patient's condition developed.

The chronology should distinguish between what the patient remembers, what the healthcare records establish, what witnesses can confirm, and what remains uncertain.

Research Note

A missing fact is not necessarily an insignificant fact. An unanswered question should be recorded and investigated rather than silently assumed.

The Role of Expert Evidence

Medical negligence litigation frequently requires expert evidence to assist the court with medical questions that fall outside ordinary knowledge.

The practitioner should nevertheless avoid treating the expert opinion as though it were the underlying fact itself.

The medical records and other evidence establish the factual foundation upon which the expert is asked to work. The expert then addresses the relevant medical questions within the expert's field.

Practitioner's Principle
Build the factual foundation before asking the expert to give an opinion. An expert cannot repair an investigation that has not established the underlying facts.

Prescription: Why the Initial Investigation Matters

The preliminary investigation also has a limitation dimension. Prescription must be considered at an early stage rather than left until litigation is about to begin.

Section 11(d) of the Prescription Act 68 of 1969 generally provides a three-year prescription period for debts for which no other period is prescribed.

Section 12(3) provides that a debt is not deemed to be due until the creditor has knowledge of the identity of the debtor and of the facts from which the debt arises, subject to the statutory reasonable-care proviso.

This makes the factual investigation important not only for the merits of the claim but also for determining when prescription may have begun to run.

Prescription and Primary Facts

In MEC for Health, Western Cape v MC , the SCA described the enquiry under section 12(3) as involving two stages.

MEC for Health, Western Cape v MC

MEC for Health, Western Cape v MC [2020] ZASCA 165

The Court explained that the enquiry first requires identification of the primary facts from which the debt arises and then asks when those facts were actually known or reasonably ought to have been known.

The Court also confirmed that a legal conclusion, such as the conclusion that negligence has been established, is not itself one of the primary facts contemplated by section 12(3).

Practitioner lesson:

Record the dates on which the client acquired the important facts, what the client knew about the relevant medical events, and what could reasonably have been discovered with reasonable care.

Links and the Knowledge Question

The Constitutional Court's decision in Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape is also important when analysing what a claimant knew or reasonably ought to have known.

Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape

Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province [2016] ZACC 10; 2016 (4) SA 414 (CC)

The case concerned prescription in a medical negligence claim and the question whether the claimant had sufficient knowledge of the relevant facts from which the debt arose.

The case is important because the prescription enquiry cannot be reduced to the simple question of whether the claimant knew the legal conclusion that the healthcare provider had been negligent.

Practitioner lesson:

When investigating prescription, ask what the client actually knew about the relevant events and their factual consequences, and what the client could reasonably have discovered through reasonable care.

Questions to Ask at the First Investigation

Facts

  • What happened?
  • When did it happen?
  • Where did it happen?
  • Who was involved?
  • What was the patient's condition beforehand?
  • What happened immediately afterwards?

Evidence

  • Which medical records exist?
  • Which records are missing?
  • Who witnessed the relevant events?
  • Which healthcare providers treated the patient afterwards?
  • What objective evidence establishes the alleged injury?

Legal investigation

  • What act or omission is alleged?
  • What legal duty or standard is potentially engaged?
  • What injury or loss is alleged?
  • What causal link is alleged?
  • Are there prescription concerns?
  • Are there statutory notice requirements that may apply?

The Practitioner’s Preliminary Test

Before moving toward litigation, ask:

  • Do I understand the client's actual complaint?
  • Have I identified the alleged act or omission?
  • Do I know the patient's condition beforehand?
  • Do I know what happened afterwards?
  • Have I identified the relevant healthcare providers?
  • Have I obtained the available medical records?
  • Have I identified the important unanswered questions?
  • Have I separated primary facts from legal conclusions?
  • Have I considered prescription?
  • Have I avoided assuming negligence merely because the outcome was poor?

The Go-Further Question

At this stage, the practitioner does not necessarily need to know whether the case will ultimately succeed.

The immediate question is whether there is a sufficiently identifiable factual basis to justify further investigation.

If important facts remain unknown, the appropriate response may be to investigate further rather than to plead prematurely.

Closing Principle
The first decision is not whether to sue. The first decision is whether the story has been investigated well enough to identify the facts, legal issues, evidence, and risks that will determine whether litigation should follow.
