Understanding the Claim

Before deciding whether a medical negligence claim should be pursued, the practitioner must first understand exactly what the client is alleging.

A patient's experience may involve a poor outcome, dissatisfaction with treatment, unexpected complications, inadequate communication, delayed treatment, or a belief that something went wrong.

These circumstances may justify investigation, but they do not automatically establish negligence.

Practitioner's Principle
First identify the complaint. Then identify the legal and factual questions that the complaint creates.

The First Task: Separate the Story From the Legal Claim

The client will usually tell the story from the perspective of what happened to them.

The practitioner's task is different. The practitioner must convert that account into a structured factual investigation.

The following questions should be identified at the beginning:

  • What treatment was provided?
  • Who provided the treatment?
  • When and where did the treatment occur?
  • What was the patient's condition before treatment?
  • What happened during or after treatment?
  • What does the patient say was done incorrectly?
  • What injury or deterioration is alleged?
  • What treatment followed?
  • What is the patient's current condition?
  • What loss is being claimed?

The Claim Map

A useful preliminary map is:

Patient → Pre-treatment condition → Medical event → Treatment → Alleged problem → Injury → Subsequent treatment → Current condition → Loss

Around this sequence, the practitioner should identify the relevant records, witnesses, healthcare providers, potential defendants, experts, dates, and unanswered questions.

Do Not Begin With the Conclusion

One of the most important disciplines in medical negligence work is avoiding premature legal conclusions.

Statements such as “the doctor was negligent,” “the hospital caused the injury,” or “the operation went wrong” are conclusions that still require investigation.

The practitioner should instead ask what specific act, omission, decision, delay, or failure is being alleged.

Practitioner's Principle
Do not allow a legal conclusion to replace a factual investigation.

What Was the Patient's Condition Before Treatment?

The patient's condition before the alleged negligent event is central to the investigation.

The practitioner should establish what symptoms, diagnosis, limitations, or disease existed before the treatment in question.

This provides the baseline against which later deterioration or injury can be considered.

  • What was the original diagnosis?
  • What symptoms existed?
  • What treatment had already been provided?
  • What was the expected course of the underlying condition?
  • What was the patient's functional status?

What Was Supposed to Happen?

The proposed treatment and the actual treatment must be distinguished.

The practitioner should determine what procedure, medication, investigation, monitoring, or intervention was planned and what was actually performed.

This may require reviewing clinical records, consent documentation, theatre records, nursing notes, medication records, imaging, and subsequent treatment records.

What Happened Afterward?

The investigation should then move forward in time.

Establish when the patient first noticed a problem, who was informed, what response followed, whether additional treatment was provided, and how the patient's condition developed.

The chronology should distinguish between what the patient remembers, what healthcare records establish, and what remains uncertain.

Research Note

A missing fact is not necessarily an insignificant fact. An unanswered question should be recorded and investigated rather than silently assumed.

The Practitioner’s Preliminary Test

Before moving toward litigation, ask:

  • Do I understand the client's actual complaint?
  • Have I identified the alleged act or omission?
  • Do I know what the patient's condition was beforehand?
  • Do I know what happened afterward?
  • Have I identified the relevant healthcare providers?
  • Have I obtained the available medical records?
  • Have I identified the important unanswered questions?
  • Have I avoided assuming negligence merely because the outcome was poor?
Closing Principle
The first decision is not whether to sue. The first decision is whether the story has been investigated well enough to identify a genuine claim.
