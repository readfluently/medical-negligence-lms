Obtaining the Medical Record

The medical record is often the first objective source from which the practitioner can reconstruct what happened to the patient.

The patient's account remains important. It identifies the story that requires investigation. But the record may contain information that the patient cannot remember, did not observe, or was not in a position to understand at the time.

Do not decide the case from the patient's recollection when the clinical record can be obtained and tested.

The medical record should therefore be treated as an investigative source, a chronology source, an evidentiary source, and a starting point for expert investigation.

Why the Record Matters

A medical-negligence claim often turns on events that occurred when the patient was unconscious, sedated, distressed, confused, or otherwise unable to observe what was happening.

The record may therefore contain the only contemporaneous account of important clinical events.

The Record May Reveal

  • The patient's condition on admission
  • Previous medical history
  • Clinical observations
  • Vital signs
  • Investigations ordered and their results
  • Diagnoses considered
  • Clinical decisions
  • Medication administered
  • Procedures performed
  • Consent documentation
  • Referrals and consultations
  • Changes in the patient's condition
  • Responses to deterioration
  • Discharge instructions
  • Follow-up treatment

The Legal Framework

The National Health Act 61 of 2003 places important obligations on health establishments concerning health records.

Section 13 requires the person in charge of a health establishment to ensure that a health record containing prescribed information is created and maintained for every user of health services.

Section 17 addresses the protection of health records and requires control measures to prevent unauthorised access to records and the systems or facilities in which they are kept.

The Act also contains provisions concerning confidentiality and access to health records.

Key statutory point: The medical record is not simply the property of the patient or an informal set of notes belonging to an individual practitioner. Health-record keeping is subject to statutory duties concerning creation, maintenance, confidentiality and protection.

Ask for the Complete Record

One of the most common investigative mistakes is requesting only the document that the patient remembers.

A request for "the doctor's notes" may produce only one component of the healthcare record.

Consider Requesting

  • Admission records
  • Clinical notes
  • Nursing notes
  • Observation charts
  • Medication charts
  • Prescription records
  • Operation or procedure notes
  • Anaesthetic records
  • Consent forms
  • Laboratory results
  • Radiology reports
  • Radiology images where relevant
  • Referral letters
  • Consultation notes
  • Discharge summaries
  • Emergency department records
  • Ambulance or transfer records
  • Physiotherapy and rehabilitation records
  • Follow-up records
  • Relevant electronic records and audit information where appropriate

The precise categories will depend on the nature of the claim.

The Record Is More Than Paper

Modern healthcare records may exist in several forms.

A patient may therefore have a paper file, an electronic clinical record, laboratory information, imaging, electronic prescriptions, nursing observations, medication administration records, and other electronically generated information.

Ask what records were created, not merely what documents the patient remembers seeing.

This distinction can become important where the alleged negligence concerns timing, communication, monitoring, medication administration, or a sequence of clinical decisions.

Do Not Assume That the First File Is the Whole File

A patient may have been treated at several facilities.

The relevant evidence may therefore be distributed across different healthcare providers.

Build the Record Trail

  • Primary care consultation
  • Referral to specialist
  • Emergency department attendance
  • Hospital admission
  • Surgery or procedure
  • Transfer to another facility
  • Discharge
  • Readmission
  • Specialist follow-up
  • Rehabilitation
  • Subsequent corrective treatment

The later records may be just as important as the original records because they can help establish what injury occurred, when it became apparent, what treatment followed, and whether another explanation for the patient's condition exists.

Read the Record Chronologically

Do not begin by searching for the sentence that appears to prove negligence.

First reconstruct the clinical sequence.

Admission → Assessment → Investigation → Decision → Treatment → Response → Deterioration or Recovery → Discharge → Follow-up

This prevents isolated entries from being removed from their clinical context.

A decision that appears unreasonable when viewed on the final page of a record may have looked different to the treating practitioner based on the information available at the time.

The question is not only what happened. It is what information was available to the practitioner when the relevant decision was made.

Separate the Record From the Interpretation

A medical record contains observations, entries, measurements, opinions, decisions and sometimes retrospective explanations.

The practitioner investigating the legal claim must distinguish those entries from the legal conclusion ultimately sought.

Ask of Each Important Entry

  • Who made the entry?
  • When was it made?
  • Was it contemporaneous?
  • What did the person actually observe?
  • What information had the person received?
  • Was the entry an observation, diagnosis, opinion or decision?
  • Was the entry later amended or supplemented?
  • Does another record confirm or contradict it?

Medical Records Are Not Automatically Proof of Everything Written in Them

A further distinction is essential.

Obtaining a medical record and proving the truth of every statement contained in that record are not the same thing.

Questions of hearsay and admissibility may arise when records are tendered as evidence. The fact that a document was discovered does not, by itself, resolve every evidentiary question.

Mooketsi v MEC for Health and Social Development

In Mooketsi v MEC for Health and Social Development , the court dealt with an application concerning the admission of medical records into evidence in terms of section 3 of the Law of Evidence Amendment Act 45 of 1988 read with section 34 of the Civil Proceedings Evidence Act 25 of 1965.

The case illustrates why the practitioner must distinguish between obtaining a document, discovering a document, and establishing the evidentiary basis on which the contents of that document may ultimately be relied upon.

Missing Medical Records

Missing records can become a serious issue in medical-negligence litigation.

The absence of a record does not automatically prove negligence. But where records that should exist are unavailable, the circumstances surrounding their absence may become legally significant.

C.B.M v MEC for Health of the Gauteng Provincial Government

In C.B.M v Member of the Executive Council for Health of the Gauteng Provincial Government , the court emphasised the importance of medical records in medical-negligence litigation.

The judgment noted that medical records are crucial to the adjudication of medical- negligence disputes and that their absence can make it difficult for experts and courts to determine what actually happened.

The court also considered the statutory duties concerning the preservation and protection of medical records under sections 13 and 17 of the National Health Act.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Important: The legal consequences of missing records depend on the circumstances. The practitioner should investigate whether the record was never created, lost, destroyed, altered, transferred, or simply remains in the possession of another institution or person.

When Records Are Missing, Ask Why

A missing record should trigger an investigation rather than an immediate conclusion.

Missing Record Investigation

  • Was the record ever created?
  • Who was responsible for keeping it?
  • Where was the patient treated?
  • Was the patient transferred?
  • Was the record transferred with the patient?
  • Has the facility conducted a search?
  • Has the search been documented?
  • Are copies held elsewhere?
  • Are electronic versions available?
  • Are there secondary records referring to the missing material?
  • Has anyone alleged that the record was destroyed?
  • If so, when and under what authority?

The Record Can Change the Case

A good preliminary investigation must allow the evidence to change the initial theory.

The patient may initially report:

"The doctor operated on me and something went wrong."

The record may reveal:

  • The complication was recognised immediately.
  • The complication was a known risk.
  • The patient's condition existed before the procedure.
  • The relevant treatment was different from what the patient remembers.
  • A different practitioner made the critical decision.
  • The alleged injury developed later.
  • Another medical event may explain the injury.
  • The patient's condition improved and subsequently deteriorated for another reason.

None of these possibilities proves that there is no claim. They demonstrate why the record must be obtained before the practitioner commits to a legal theory.

Build the Record-Based Chronology

Once the records are obtained, convert them into a chronology.

Chronology Fields

  • Date and time
  • Healthcare provider
  • Patient's condition
  • Investigation
  • Clinical finding
  • Decision
  • Treatment
  • Response
  • Next decision
  • Unanswered question

The chronology should distinguish between what the record establishes and what remains uncertain.

A good chronology does not hide uncertainty. It exposes it.

The Record and Expert Evidence

Experts should not be asked to evaluate a case from a selective collection of documents when the underlying clinical record can reasonably be obtained.

An expert opinion based on incomplete records may be weakened if important clinical facts later emerge.

The importance of records to expert evidence was emphasised in C.B.M v MEC for Health of the Gauteng Provincial Government .

The court noted that the absence of medical records can limit an expert's ability to give a definitive opinion on the actual cause of an alleged injury.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Practitioner lesson: Give the expert the clinical story as completely as reasonably possible. Do not construct the expert brief around only those documents that support the client's allegation.

The Medical Record and the Standard of Care

The record may identify the clinical decision that requires examination, but it does not automatically establish that the decision was negligent.

For example, a record may show:

"Patient deteriorated at 14:00. Doctor reviewed patient at 14:20 and ordered further investigation."

That entry identifies an event.

The legal investigation still has to ask whether the response was reasonable in the circumstances, what information was available, what a reasonable practitioner in the relevant field would have done, and whether any alleged departure caused the injury.

The record supplies facts. The standard of care requires clinical interpretation.

The Medical Record and Causation

The record should also be examined for the development of the alleged injury.

Establish:

  • When was the patient first documented as normal or stable?
  • When was the first abnormal finding?
  • When was the alleged injury first identified?
  • What happened immediately before the injury?
  • What happened afterwards?
  • What treatment was given for the injury?
  • Did the injury change over time?
  • Are there competing explanations?
  • What is the patient's present condition?

This prevents causation from being reduced to a simple sequence:

"Treatment happened, then injury happened, therefore treatment caused injury."

Temporal sequence is important, but it is not necessarily sufficient to establish legal causation.

Preserve the Original Investigation

When records are received, preserve what was actually received.

Record Handling

  • Keep the original electronic files where supplied electronically.
  • Keep complete copies of paper records.
  • Record the date the records were received.
  • Record from whom they were received.
  • Do not silently remove pages.
  • Do not alter the original records.
  • Keep a working copy for annotations and analysis.
  • Identify missing pages or unexplained gaps.
  • Record duplicate documents rather than assuming duplication is meaningless.

The objective is to preserve an accurate evidentiary trail from the first investigation onwards.

The Practitioner’s Record Audit

Before Moving Forward

  • Have all healthcare facilities involved been identified?
  • Have records been requested from each relevant facility?
  • Have both paper and electronic records been considered?
  • Have nursing and allied-health records been obtained where relevant?
  • Have investigation results been obtained?
  • Have imaging reports and relevant images been considered?
  • Have medication and procedure records been obtained?
  • Have consent records been obtained where relevant?
  • Have subsequent treatment records been obtained?
  • Have missing records been identified?
  • Has the reason for missing records been investigated?
  • Has a chronology been created?
  • Have contradictions between records been identified?
  • Have unanswered clinical questions been recorded?

The Go-Further Question

Once the complete record has been obtained, the investigation should not immediately jump to the conclusion that negligence has been established.

The next task is to test the factual allegations against the clinical record.

What does the record establish, what does it contradict, and what does it leave unanswered?

That is the bridge between collecting evidence and testing the facts.
