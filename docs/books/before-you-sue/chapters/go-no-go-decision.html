The Go / No-Go Decision

The preliminary investigation should eventually produce a decision. Not necessarily a decision to litigate immediately, but a reasoned decision about what should happen next.

Practitioner Principle: Do not issue proceedings simply because the patient suffered a serious injury. Proceed when the available evidence supports a coherent case on duty, breach, causation and damage, and when the procedural position permits the claim to be pursued.

The purpose of this chapter is therefore not to produce certainty. It is to determine whether the investigation has reached a point at which the practitioner can responsibly say:

  • Go: the matter is sufficiently supported to proceed;
  • Investigate Further: important questions remain unresolved; or
  • No-Go: the available evidence does not justify pursuing the claim.

What the Decision Is — and Is Not

The Go / No-Go decision is not a prediction of what a court will ultimately decide.

A practitioner making the preliminary assessment is working with incomplete evidence. Some medical records may still be outstanding. Experts may not yet have reported. The patient's recollection may require testing against the records. The precise causal mechanism may still need investigation.

The question is therefore not: “Can I guarantee that the patient will win?”

The better question is: “Do I presently have a sufficiently supported legal and factual proposition to justify taking the next step?”

The Claim Must Still Have a Coherent Structure

By this stage, the practitioner should be able to describe the proposed claim without relying entirely on conclusions.

The Core Claim

  • What was the patient's condition before the treatment or event?
  • What treatment, investigation or decision was made?
  • What should reasonably have happened?
  • What actually happened?
  • What conduct is alleged to have fallen below the required standard?
  • Who is legally responsible for that conduct?
  • What injury or loss followed?
  • What evidence connects the alleged breach to the injury?

If these questions cannot yet be answered, the matter may belong in the Investigate Further category rather than the Go category.

First Gate: Is There a Defensible Factual Foundation?

A claim should not be built exclusively on the patient's conclusion that “the doctor made a mistake”.

The practitioner should have identified the underlying facts and tested them against the available records and other evidence.

A useful distinction is:

  • Allegation: what the patient says happened;
  • Fact: what the available evidence establishes;
  • Inference: what can reasonably be concluded from those facts;
  • Legal proposition: why those facts may amount to actionable conduct.

The stronger the case becomes at each level, the more confidently the practitioner can move towards a Go decision.

Second Gate: Is the Defendant Correctly Identified?

The person who treated the patient is not necessarily the only or even the correct defendant.

The investigation should already have considered whether the proposed claim concerns:

  • an individual practitioner;
  • a hospital or private healthcare institution;
  • a provincial or national department;
  • an organ of state;
  • an employer or principal;
  • another healthcare professional; or
  • more than one potentially responsible party.

The defendant analysis should be based on the facts of the particular matter and the legal basis of responsibility. It should not simply follow the name appearing on the patient's complaint.

Third Gate: Is There a Standard-of-Care Proposition?

A medical-negligence claim requires more than showing that the outcome was poor.

The practitioner should be able to formulate a specific proposition about what reasonable care required in the circumstances.

Useful formulation: “In the circumstances then prevailing, a reasonably competent practitioner exercising reasonable skill and care would probably have taken X step, avoided Y conduct, or responded to Z finding in a different manner.”

The proposition must then be tested against the clinical circumstances, the available evidence and, where necessary, appropriate expert opinion.

The practitioner should avoid a formulation based purely on hindsight: the fact that the patient suffered harm does not itself establish that the earlier clinical decision was negligent.

Fourth Gate: Is the Expert Evidence Sufficient?

Medical negligence disputes frequently turn on questions that cannot responsibly be resolved from the patient's account alone.

The expert evidence should therefore be tested for more than the conclusion it reaches.

Expert Evidence Audit

  • Has the expert been given the relevant medical records?
  • Has the expert considered the patient's condition before the disputed event?
  • Has the expert identified the precise clinical decision or omission in issue?
  • Has the expert explained the applicable standard?
  • Has the expert distinguished an adverse outcome from negligent conduct?
  • Has the expert addressed causation?
  • Has the expert considered reasonable alternative explanations?
  • Is the opinion supported by the facts and clinical records?

In Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another, the Supreme Court of Appeal emphasised the importance of evaluating expert opinion by reference to its logical basis and the reasons supporting the opinion.

An expert's professional status does not make the conclusion immune from scrutiny.

Fifth Gate: Is Causation Established on the Available Evidence?

Even if negligent conduct can be identified, the claim does not automatically succeed.

The practitioner must separately investigate whether the alleged breach caused the harm for which compensation is sought.

Factual causation ordinarily involves the “but-for” enquiry: what probably would have happened if the alleged wrongful conduct had not occurred?

The enquiry is practical and probabilistic. It does not require scientific certainty, but the causal proposition must be supported by the evidence.

The investigation should also consider legal causation — whether the harm is sufficiently closely connected to the wrongful conduct for legal liability to follow.

International Shipping Co (Pty) Ltd v Bentley remains a central authority for the distinction between factual and legal causation.

Do Not Collapse Negligence and Causation Into One Question

One of the most dangerous shortcuts in medical-negligence litigation is the assumption:

“The practitioner should have done something differently, and the patient was injured, therefore the different conduct caused the injury.”

Those are separate propositions.

The practitioner should instead be able to identify:

  1. what should have been done;
  2. what was actually done or omitted;
  3. why that conduct fell below the required standard;
  4. what probably would have happened if reasonable care had been provided; and
  5. what loss followed from the difference between those courses.

Sixth Gate: What Is the Damage?

The existence of an injury does not by itself establish every item of loss claimed.

The investigation should identify the actual consequences of the alleged negligence and distinguish them from:

  • the patient's pre-existing condition;
  • the natural progression of the underlying disease;
  • harm that would probably have occurred regardless of the alleged breach;
  • independent subsequent events; and
  • loss that has not yet been supported by evidence.

At this stage, the practitioner does not necessarily need a final quantum calculation. The important question is whether there is a sufficiently identifiable loss connected to the proposed cause of action.

Seventh Gate: Was There Informed Consent?

Consent must be analysed separately from negligent treatment.

A patient may have consented to an operation or treatment and still potentially have a claim arising from negligent performance or negligent clinical decision-making.

Conversely, where the complaint concerns failure to disclose a material risk, the practitioner must investigate the consent process itself, including the information provided, the risk in question, the patient's understanding and whether the alleged non-disclosure was causally connected to the harm.

A signed consent form should therefore be treated as evidence of the consent process, not automatically as a complete answer to every allegation arising from the treatment.

Eighth Gate: Has Prescription Been Properly Analysed?

Prescription should be treated as an active part of the preliminary investigation rather than an administrative issue to be considered immediately before summons.

For an ordinary delictual medical-negligence claim, the applicable prescription period and the date on which the debt became due must be identified from the particular legal and factual circumstances.

Section 12(3) of the Prescription Act is particularly important because it addresses when a debt is regarded as due where the creditor does not yet have knowledge of the identity of the debtor and the facts from which the debt arises, subject to the reasonable-care qualification.

The practitioner should therefore record:

  • the relevant date or period of the alleged negligence;
  • when the patient knew or could reasonably have acquired knowledge of the relevant facts;
  • when the identity of the debtor became known or reasonably discoverable;
  • any possible interruption of prescription; and
  • the date on which proceedings must be instituted if prescription is approaching.

A claim should never be classified as safe merely because the patient believes that “three years have not yet passed”.

Ninth Gate: Is the Defendant an Organ of State?

Where the proposed defendant is an organ of state, the practitioner must separately consider the Institution of Legal Proceedings Against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002.

Section 3 generally requires written notice of intended legal proceedings to be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due, subject to the statutory provisions concerning knowledge, consent and condonation.

The notice must briefly set out the facts giving rise to the debt and the particulars of the debt within the creditor's knowledge.

Failure to comply should not simply be treated as fatal without considering the statutory condonation mechanism. Section 3(4) provides for an application for condonation where the statutory requirements are satisfied, including that the debt has not been extinguished by prescription, that good cause exists for the failure, and that the organ of state was not unreasonably prejudiced.

Practitioner Warning: The six-month statutory notice period and prescription are different questions. A practitioner should calculate and record both separately.

The Difference Between “Go” and “Investigate Further”

Not every unresolved issue requires abandonment of the claim.

Some uncertainties are simply evidence gaps that can reasonably be addressed.

Investigate Further Where:

  • important medical records are still outstanding;
  • the correct defendant has not been established;
  • the clinical chronology contains unexplained contradictions;
  • the standard of care cannot yet be formulated precisely;
  • an expert opinion is required but has not yet been obtained;
  • causation remains uncertain because essential evidence is missing;
  • the extent of the injury requires further medical assessment;
  • prescription or statutory notice dates require further factual investigation; or
  • the available evidence points in competing directions that have not yet been tested.

When the Case Should Move Toward “No-Go”

A responsible practitioner should also be prepared to stop a claim.

A matter may be unsuitable to pursue where, after reasonable investigation:

  • the alleged breach cannot be identified;
  • the conduct appears consistent with reasonable clinical practice;
  • the proposed standard is unsupported;
  • the expert evidence does not support negligence;
  • the injury would probably have occurred despite reasonable care;
  • the causal connection is too speculative;
  • the alleged loss cannot reasonably be connected to the claim;
  • the claim is prescribed and no viable legal route remains; or
  • the available evidence is materially inconsistent with the proposed case.

Stopping a weak claim is not a failure of representation. It is part of responsible litigation practice.

The Three-Way Decision

At the end of the preliminary investigation, the practitioner should be able to place the matter into one of three categories.

GO

The core facts are sufficiently established. The defendant has been identified. A defensible standard-of-care proposition exists. Expert evidence supports the relevant medical propositions where required. Causation and damage have a reasonable evidentiary foundation. Prescription and statutory notice have been addressed.

INVESTIGATE FURTHER

The claim may be viable, but one or more material questions remain unresolved. The next investigative step is identifiable and proportionate.

NO-GO

The investigation does not presently support a viable claim, and the remaining weaknesses cannot reasonably be cured by further investigation.

The Preliminary Case Assessment

Before moving beyond the pre-litigation stage, the practitioner should be able to prepare a concise written assessment of the case.

Case Assessment

  • Patient: Who is the claimant?
  • Defendant: Who is legally implicated and why?
  • Event: What treatment, omission or decision is challenged?
  • Breach: What was the required standard and how was it allegedly breached?
  • Evidence: Which records and witnesses support the proposition?
  • Expert: What expert evidence is available or still required?
  • Causation: What probably would have happened with reasonable care?
  • Damage: What injury and loss are attributed to the alleged breach?
  • Consent: Is informed consent a separate issue?
  • Prescription: When does the debt become due and when does prescription expire?
  • Notice: Does an organ-of-state notice requirement apply?
  • Decision: Go, Investigate Further or No-Go?

The Decision Should Be Recorded

The Go / No-Go decision should not exist only as an informal conversation between practitioner and client.

A short written assessment creates a record of the reasoning that led to the decision and identifies the assumptions that still require testing.

Where the decision is to investigate further, the practitioner should identify the precise outstanding question and the next step required to answer it.

Better question: “What evidence would change our present assessment?”

That question keeps the investigation evidence-driven rather than allowing the case theory to become fixed before the evidence is complete.

The Final Pre-Litigation Test

Before proceedings are contemplated, the practitioner should be able to tell the story of the claim in a disciplined sequence:

  1. The patient had a particular condition or clinical circumstance.
  2. The practitioner or institution owed a relevant legal duty.
  3. A particular act or omission is alleged to have fallen below the required standard.
  4. The allegation is supported by evidence rather than hindsight alone.
  5. The alleged breach probably caused the relevant harm.
  6. The harm produced legally recognisable loss.
  7. The correct defendant or defendants have been identified.
  8. Consent and risk issues have been separately analysed where relevant.
  9. Prescription has been calculated.
  10. Any statutory notice requirement has been identified and addressed.
  11. The available expert evidence is sufficiently reasoned and supported.

If the practitioner cannot yet tell that story coherently, the answer may still be “Investigate Further”.

The Central Principle of Book 2

Do not litigate the patient's story before investigating the patient's case.

The purpose of the pre-litigation investigation is to transform a complaint into a tested legal proposition.

By the end of this book, the practitioner should not merely know that the patient was harmed. The practitioner should know what happened, who may be responsible, what the applicable standard may have required, whether that standard was probably breached, whether the breach probably caused the harm, what loss resulted, and whether the claim can still properly be pursued.

Only then is it sensible to move from preliminary investigation toward the deeper evidentiary work required for litigation.
