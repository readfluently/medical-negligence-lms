Expert Opinions

Expert evidence is often where a medical-negligence investigation moves from the patient's story and medical records into the specialised questions that ultimately have to be proved.

Practitioner's principle: Do not begin by asking, “Which expert should I appoint?” Begin by asking, “What proposition requires specialised knowledge?”

The source manuscript treats expert selection as a later stage of the investigation. At the first consultation, the practitioner should identify potential areas of expertise rather than immediately commission opinions. The questions come first; the expert comes second.

1. Start With the Question, Not the Specialty

One of the most important practical disciplines in a medical-negligence matter is to identify precisely what specialised question needs to be answered.

The manuscript gives examples such as:

Was the patient's presentation consistent with condition X?

What investigation would reasonably have been indicated?

Should the patient have been referred?

Was the treatment appropriate?

Would earlier intervention probably have altered the outcome?

What is the patient's present prognosis?

Question requiring specialised knowledge

The starting point should be the proposition that cannot responsibly be answered through ordinary legal analysis alone.

The practitioner should identify the precise medical or technical question before deciding who is best qualified to answer it.

For example, “Was the doctor negligent?” may be too broad as an instruction. More useful questions may include:

  • What symptoms were clinically significant?
  • What investigations were reasonably indicated?
  • What diagnosis should reasonably have been considered?
  • What treatment options were available?
  • What risks and benefits attached to those options?
  • Would earlier intervention probably have changed the outcome?

Once the specialised proposition is identified, the practitioner can determine which discipline is capable of addressing it.

See Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another [2001] ZASCA 12 on the evaluation of expert opinions and the need for logical reasoning.

2. Potential Expert

Potential expert

A potential expert is not simply a medical practitioner whose title appears relevant to the case.

The more useful starting point is the proposition that requires specialised knowledge.

For example, neurological injury following surgery may involve several distinct questions:

  • What happened during the operation?
  • Was the anaesthetic management appropriate?
  • What neurological injury occurred?
  • When did the injury probably occur?
  • Could earlier intervention have prevented or reduced it?
  • What are the long-term functional consequences?

Those questions may not all belong to one expert. The appropriate expert team depends upon the propositions that must be established.

At the first consultation, the practitioner is therefore identifying possible areas of expertise rather than automatically commissioning reports.

3. Expert Evidence Is Not the Case

An expert assists the court. The expert does not decide the litigation.

This distinction is fundamental. A practitioner should therefore be cautious when an expert report appears to contain conclusions such as “the defendant was negligent” without properly explaining the medical facts, professional standard and reasoning that support the opinion.

Expert evidence

Expert evidence is evidence intended to assist the court on matters requiring specialised knowledge outside ordinary judicial experience.

The Supreme Court of Appeal in Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another [2001] ZASCA 12 held that the court must evaluate whether expert opinions are founded on logical reasoning.

The court is therefore not required simply to choose whichever expert sounds more persuasive. The reasoning, factual foundation and logic of the opinion must be examined.

Key proposition: An expert opinion must have a logical basis and be capable of providing genuine assistance to the court.

See also Life Healthcare Group (Pty) Ltd v Suliman [2018] ZASCA 118 , which reaffirmed that the ultimate evaluation of expert opinion is for the court.

4. Independence and Objectivity

Expert independence and objectivity

An expert may be instructed by one of the parties, but that does not turn the expert into an advocate.

The expert should provide an objective and unbiased opinion based upon specialised knowledge and the available evidence.

In Schneider NO and Others v Aspeling and Another [2010] ZAWCHC 3 , the court stressed that an expert must remain objective, identify matters outside the expert's expertise and disclose when the opinion is provisional because insufficient information is available.

This is especially important in medical-negligence litigation where the expert may be dealing with incomplete records or disputed factual assumptions.

Practitioner test: Ask whether the report would still appear professionally defensible if it were being read by the opposing party.

5. The Factual Foundation of the Opinion

A sophisticated medical opinion cannot repair an unreliable factual foundation.

Factual basis

The factual basis of an expert opinion consists of the records, information, assumptions and other evidence from which the expert's reasoning proceeds.

The practitioner should therefore ask:

  • What records did the expert receive?
  • Which facts did the expert assume?
  • Which facts were actually established?
  • Are any important records missing?
  • Does the expert distinguish fact from assumption?
  • Does the opinion change depending on disputed facts?

The Supreme Court of Appeal has repeatedly emphasised that the reasoning behind an expert opinion must be disclosed so that the court can test it.

See Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 and Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

6. Standard of Care

Standard of care

The standard of care asks what level of skill and care could reasonably be expected of a practitioner in the relevant circumstances and field.

The expert should not simply say: “I would have done something different.”

The useful opinion should explain:

  • what clinical circumstances existed;
  • what options were reasonably available;
  • what risks and benefits were involved;
  • what a reasonably competent practitioner would have considered;
  • what was actually done; and
  • why the conduct did or did not fall below the relevant standard.

The Linksfield approach remains central: Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

See also Cheminais v Crane [2025] ZAWCHC 183 on logical reasoning and defensible expert opinion.

7. Expert Evidence on Causation

Expert evidence on causation

An expert who is well placed to discuss diagnosis or treatment may not necessarily be the expert best placed to address every causation issue.

The practitioner should separate:

  • what caused the injury;
  • when the injury probably occurred;
  • whether the alleged breach contributed to the injury;
  • whether another cause better explains the outcome; and
  • whether earlier treatment would probably have changed the outcome.

These propositions may require different disciplines.

See Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape [2015] ZACC 33 for important Constitutional Court discussion of medical negligence, expert evidence and causation.

Practitioner warning: Do not ask an expert only whether the defendant was negligent. Ask the expert to identify the medical proposition that allows the court to address that issue.

8. Drafting the Instruction to the Expert

The instruction letter forms part of the architecture of the evidence. A vague instruction is likely to produce a vague report.

Expert instruction

A proper instruction should identify the relevant factual history, records supplied and questions requiring specialised opinion without dictating the answer.

A useful instruction may ask:

Was the presentation consistent with the diagnosis later made?

What investigations would reasonably have been considered at that stage?

Was referral to another practitioner or facility indicated?

Was the treatment provided appropriate in the circumstances?

What, in your opinion, caused the injury?

If the patient had received the alternative intervention at the identified time, what outcome was medically probable?

The medical reasoning should remain the expert's own.

Good instruction: identifies the proposition to be answered.

Poor instruction: tells the expert what legal conclusion the practitioner wants.

9. Give the Expert the Right Material

The practitioner should not assume that “the hospital file” is everything the expert needs.

Depending on the case, the material may include:

  • hospital records;
  • nursing notes;
  • theatre records;
  • anaesthetic records;
  • laboratory results;
  • radiology reports and images;
  • previous medical records;
  • subsequent treatment records;
  • rehabilitation records;
  • relevant witness information; and
  • other expert reports where appropriate.

Material supplied to the expert

The objective is not simply to send a large bundle. The objective is to provide the material necessary for the expert to answer the particular question reliably.

The practitioner should consider whether the material gives the expert access to:

  • the patient's condition before the event;
  • the relevant clinical decision-making;
  • the treatment actually provided;
  • the development of the injury;
  • subsequent treatment; and
  • the present condition.

Adverse records and contradictory entries should not simply disappear from the expert's bundle.

Do not hide the difficult material. An adverse record, contradictory entry or previous medical condition may be critical to the reliability of the opinion.

10. The Medico-Legal Report

Medico-legal report

A useful report should allow the court and opposing parties to understand the expert's expertise, information considered, assumptions, reasoning and conclusions.

The practitioner should be able to work through the report and ask:

  • What question was the expert asked?
  • What material did the expert consider?
  • What facts were assumed?
  • What medical principles were applied?
  • What alternative explanations were considered?
  • Why was one explanation preferred?
  • How strong is the conclusion?
  • What limitations affect the opinion?

In Schneider , the court stressed that an expert should identify limitations where insufficient information prevents a firm opinion.

11. Rule 36(9) and Expert Evidence

Once litigation begins, expert evidence is governed not only by principles of evidence but also by procedural rules.

Rule 36(9)

Rule 36(9) of the Uniform Rules of Court regulates the giving of notice concerning expert witnesses and the delivery of summaries of expert opinion.

Under the current High Court framework, the general time periods are different for plaintiffs and defendants and are linked to the close of pleadings and the case-management process.

The practitioner should therefore not treat an old textbook timetable as automatically applicable to a current matter.

The procedural notice identifying an intention to call an expert witness.

Expert notice

In High Court litigation, the applicable rules and case-management directions determine when the notice must be delivered.

The practitioner should record the deadline immediately on the litigation diary and check the current rule, directive and case-management order.


The procedural summary explaining the substance and reasons for an expert's opinion.

Expert summary

The summary should enable the opposing side and court to understand the material opinions the expert is expected to give.

It should not be approached as a formality. The summary is part of the litigation architecture and should accurately identify the opinions that will be relied upon.

Important: Procedural deadlines must be checked against the current applicable Uniform Rules, Magistrates' Court Rules where relevant, case-management order and any current directives. The High Court and Magistrates' Court systems are not interchangeable.

12. Expert Meetings and Joint Minutes

Where both sides have experts addressing the same issue, the experts may be required to meet and identify what they agree and what remains disputed.

Joint minutes

A joint minute should reduce uncertainty and identify clearly the matters that remain disputed.

It may identify:

  • what the experts agree upon;
  • what they do not agree upon;
  • the reasons for disagreement; and
  • whether the disagreement concerns fact, interpretation or opinion.

See Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 on the importance and evidential significance of expert joint minutes.

13. What the Experts Actually Agree About

The practitioner should distinguish carefully between an agreement about a fact and an agreement about an expert opinion.

Agreed factual proposition

This refers to an underlying factual proposition that the experts accept as common ground for purposes of their analysis.

Examples might include the timing of an event, a recorded test result, or a particular clinical finding, depending upon what is actually established in the evidence.

The practitioner should still examine the evidential foundation of the agreement and ensure that the agreed fact is not based merely on an incorrect assumption.

Agreed expert opinion

This refers to an expert conclusion that both sides' experts accept after considering the relevant material.

Such agreement can materially narrow the issues, but the practitioner should still understand exactly what has been agreed and what proposition remains for the court to determine.

See Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 on the importance of agreements reached by experts in joint minutes.

14. Conflicting Experts

A medical-negligence trial may involve genuinely competing professional opinions.

The existence of disagreement does not mean that one expert must simply be preferred because of status or confidence.

Conflicting experts

Where experts disagree, the court examines the reasoning underlying the competing opinions and considers those opinions against the objective facts and probabilities of the case.

The foundational authority remains Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

A later example is Hassiem v MEC for Health, Western Cape Provincial Health Department [2026] ZAWCHC 463 , where the court again applied the Linksfield approach to competing medical opinions.

Litigation lesson: Do not ask merely, “Whose expert is right?” Ask, “Which opinion is supported by the facts, assumptions, methodology and reasoning?”

15. The Unfavourable Expert Opinion

One of the most difficult moments in a medical-negligence practice is receiving an opinion that does not support the client's case.

Unfavourable opinion

An unfavourable opinion is information about the strength of the case. It should be analysed before deciding what litigation strategy follows.

Ask:

  • Is the expert addressing the correct question?
  • Was the complete medical record available?
  • Did the expert misunderstand a factual issue?
  • Are the assumptions correct?
  • Has contrary evidence been considered?
  • Is the conclusion stronger than the reasoning supports?
  • Is the real disagreement medical or legal?

Practitioner discipline: The purpose of the expert process is to discover what the evidence supports, not to manufacture an opinion that supports the client.

16. When One Expert Is Not Enough

A complex injury may cross several specialised disciplines.

The manuscript specifically identifies the possibility of building an expert team rather than searching for one “master expert.”

Expert team

An expert team is useful where separate specialised propositions cannot responsibly be answered by one discipline.

Question Possible expertise
Was the surgery appropriately performed? Relevant surgical specialist
Was anaesthetic management appropriate? Anaesthetist
What injury occurred? Relevant clinical specialist
When did the injury probably occur? Relevant clinical specialist
What is the functional impact? Rehabilitation or occupational expertise
What is the vocational consequence? Industrial psychology
How should future loss be quantified? Actuarial expertise

The disciplines should not be selected merely because they appear in a familiar medico-legal template. Each expert should have a defined forensic purpose.

17. Preparing the Expert for Trial

A strong written report does not automatically produce strong oral evidence.

Before the expert enters the witness box

  • Review the report paragraph by paragraph.
  • Identify every material assumption.
  • Identify the strongest contrary evidence.
  • Know which opinions are firm and which are qualified.
  • Check the expert's understanding of the records.
  • Review the opposing expert's report.
  • Review the joint minute carefully.
  • Identify what has been agreed and what remains disputed.

Preparing the expert for trial

Trial preparation should not involve coaching an expert to give evidence favourable to the client.

It should involve ensuring that the expert understands the evidence, assumptions, limits of the opinion and areas of disagreement.

What is this expert actually proving?

What evidence supports the proposition?

What is the strongest argument against it?

18. Reading an Expert Report as a Practitioner

The practitioner should not read a medico-legal report passively. Read it as though preparing the case for cross-examination.

Report review questions

  • Question: What exactly was the expert asked?
  • Material: What records and information were considered?
  • Facts: Which facts are proved, disputed or assumed?
  • Method: How did the expert reason from facts to conclusion?
  • Alternatives: What alternative explanations were considered?
  • Conclusion: Is the conclusion definitive, probable, possible or provisional?
  • Limitations: What information would change the opinion?

Reasoning behind the opinion

The expert's conclusion should not appear as an unexplained assertion.

The court must be able to understand the route by which the expert moved from the facts and specialised knowledge to the ultimate opinion.

This principle is central to Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

19. The Cost of Expert Evidence

Expert evidence can become one of the most expensive components of medical-negligence litigation.

Forensic purpose

A forensic purpose is the specific reason an item of expert evidence is needed in the litigation.

Before incurring an expert cost, the practitioner should be able to say exactly what proposition the report is intended to establish or test.

The question is not simply: “Does this type of case usually have this expert?”

The better question is: “What issue in this case requires this expert's evidence?”

Before incurring an expert cost

  • What proposition does this expert address?
  • Is the proposition genuinely disputed?
  • Could an existing expert answer it?
  • What records are necessary?
  • Is the issue liability, causation or quantum?
  • Will the opinion materially affect litigation strategy?

21. Practitioner’s Expert File

Expert file checklist

  • The specialised proposition has been identified.
  • The expert's discipline is appropriate to that proposition.
  • The expert's qualifications and experience have been considered.
  • The expert has received the relevant records.
  • Material adverse information has not been withheld.
  • The instruction identifies the questions requiring an opinion.
  • The report identifies material assumptions.
  • The expert explains the reasoning supporting material conclusions.
  • The expert identifies limitations and uncertainty.
  • Rule 36(9) requirements have been considered.
  • The opposing expert report has been reviewed.
  • The joint minute has been reviewed.
  • Areas of agreement and disagreement are identified.
  • The expert evidence has been integrated into the pleaded case.

22. Interactive Exercise: Choose the Expert

Scenario

A patient undergoes surgery and later develops severe neurological impairment.

The patient alleges that something went wrong during the operation.

Possible experts include:

  • the surgeon;
  • the anaesthetist;
  • the neurologist;
  • the radiologist;
  • the rehabilitation specialist;
  • the occupational therapist;
  • the industrial psychologist; and
  • the actuary.

Question 1

Which expert would address whether the surgery itself was appropriately performed?

Reveal discussion

The relevant surgical discipline would ordinarily address that proposition. The exact specialty depends upon the procedure in issue.

Question 2

Which expert would address whether anaesthetic management was appropriate?

Reveal discussion

An anaesthetist would ordinarily be the relevant discipline for questions concerning anaesthetic management.

Question 3

Which expert might address the nature and consequences of the neurological injury?

Reveal discussion

A neurologist or another appropriately qualified specialist may be required, depending upon the exact injury and proposition in issue.

Question 4

Which experts may be required to address future functional consequences and loss of earning capacity?

Reveal discussion

The answer may involve several disciplines. Functional consequences may require rehabilitation or occupational expertise; vocational consequences may require industrial psychology; and quantification may require actuarial expertise.

23. The Expert Question Test

Before appointing any expert, complete this sentence:

“I need this expert because the court must decide whether ______.”

Expert question test

The test forces the practitioner to identify the proposition for which specialised evidence is actually necessary.

Then complete:

“The expert will assist the court by explaining ______.”

If that sentence cannot be completed clearly, the practitioner may not yet have identified the issue requiring expert evidence.

24. Leading Authorities for Further Research

  • Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another [2001] ZASCA 12; 2001 (3) SA 1188 (SCA) — foundational authority on evaluating expert opinion and logical reasoning.
  • Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape [2015] ZACC 33 — Constitutional Court authority involving expert evidence, medical negligence and causation.
  • Schneider NO and Others v Aspeling and Another [2010] ZAWCHC 3 — important authority on expert independence, objectivity and the factual foundation of expert evidence.
  • Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 — important authority on expert reasoning and joint minutes.
  • Life Healthcare Group (Pty) Ltd v Suliman [2018] ZASCA 118 — reaffirmed the court's responsibility to evaluate competing expert evidence.
  • Cheminais v Crane [2025] ZAWCHC 183 — recent application of the Linksfield approach to logical expert reasoning.
  • Hassiem v MEC for Health, Western Cape Provincial Health Department [2026] ZAWCHC 463 — recent application of the approach to competing medical opinions.
Research note: Procedural requirements for expert evidence should always be checked against the current applicable rules, directives and case-management order. This chapter is a practitioner guide, not a substitute for checking the procedural regime governing the particular matter.

25. The Practitioner’s Test

Do not appoint an expert because the case “needs an expert.”

Appoint an expert because a specific proposition requires specialised knowledge and that proposition matters to the outcome of the case.

What the expert is actually there to prove

Before trial, the practitioner should know exactly what each expert is there to establish.

The expert should have a defined proposition, the necessary material, an independent reasoning process and a conclusion that fits within the evidential structure of the case.

The opposing opinion should be tested.

The joint minute should narrow the issues.

And the practitioner should understand exactly what remains for the court to decide.
