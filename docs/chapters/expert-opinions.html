Expert Opinions

Expert evidence is often where a medical-negligence investigation moves from the patient's story and medical records into the specialised questions that ultimately have to be proved.

Practitioner's principle: Do not begin by asking, “Which expert should I appoint?” Begin by asking, “What proposition requires specialised knowledge?”

The source manuscript treats expert selection as a later stage of the investigation. At the first consultation, the practitioner should identify potential areas of expertise rather than immediately commission opinions. The questions come first; the expert comes second.

FACTS ↓ MEDICAL RECORDS ↓ LEGAL QUESTIONS ↓ SPECIALISED PROPOSITION ↓ APPROPRIATE EXPERT ↓ REASONED OPINION ↓ EVIDENCE AT TRIAL

1. Start With the Question, Not the Specialty

One of the most important practical disciplines in a medical-negligence matter is to identify precisely what specialised question needs to be answered.

The manuscript gives examples such as:

Was the patient's presentation consistent with condition X?

What investigation would reasonably have been indicated?

Should the patient have been referred?

Was the treatment appropriate?

Would earlier intervention probably have altered the outcome?

What is the patient's present prognosis?

Only after identifying those propositions should the practitioner determine which expert discipline is capable of addressing them.

Potential expert

A potential expert is not simply a medical practitioner whose title appears relevant to the case. The more useful starting point is the proposition that requires specialised knowledge.

For example, neurological injury following surgery may involve several distinct questions:

  • What happened during the operation?
  • Was the anaesthetic management appropriate?
  • What neurological injury occurred?
  • When did the injury probably occur?
  • Could earlier intervention have prevented or reduced it?
  • What are the long-term functional consequences?

Those questions may not all belong to one expert. The appropriate expert team depends upon the propositions that must be established.

Practitioner use: Write the question in one sentence before selecting the expert.

The Constitutional Court in Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape [2015] ZACC 33 considered competing expert evidence in a medical-negligence dispute and emphasised the importance of proper evaluation of the reasoning underlying expert opinions.

2. Expert Evidence Is Not the Case

An expert assists the court. The expert does not decide the litigation.

This distinction is fundamental. A practitioner should therefore be cautious when an expert report appears to contain conclusions such as “the defendant was negligent” without properly explaining the medical facts, assumptions, professional standard and reasoning that support the opinion.

Expert evidence

Expert evidence is evidence intended to assist the court on matters requiring specialised knowledge that falls outside ordinary judicial experience.

The Supreme Court of Appeal in Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another [2001] ZASCA 12 made clear that the ultimate question of reasonableness and negligence belongs to the court. The court evaluates the competing expert opinions and the reasoning underlying them before reaching its own conclusion.

This means that an expert's status, reputation or confidence is not a substitute for reasoning.

Key proposition: The court must determine whether and to what extent the opinions advanced are founded on logical reasoning.

This approach has repeatedly been reaffirmed in later medical-negligence litigation, including Oppelt and more recent decisions such as K.E.M obo O.M v MEC for Health, North West Provincial Government [2026] ZANWHC 139 .

3. Independence and Objectivity

An expert is retained by a party, but the expert's function in court is not to become an advocate for that party.

Expert independence and objectivity

The expert should provide an independent, objective opinion based on the available facts and specialised knowledge.

The classic formulation appears in Schneider NO and Others v Aspeling and Another [2010] ZAWCHC 3 . The court stressed the need for the expert's evidence to be independent and objective, with the expert stating the facts or assumptions on which the opinion is based.

The expert should also identify matters falling outside the expert's field and should state when insufficient information prevents a firm conclusion.

That is particularly important in medical-negligence litigation because the quality of an opinion may depend heavily on the quality and completeness of the medical records supplied to the expert.

Practitioner test: Ask whether the report would still appear professionally defensible if it were being read by the opposing party rather than the instructing attorney.

A recent 2026 decision, L.P.M obo L.M v MEC for Health: North West Provincial Government [2026] ZANWHC 92 , again emphasised objectivity, the need to identify relevant knowledge and experience, and the requirement that an expert not assume the role of advocate.

4. The Factual Foundation of the Opinion

A sophisticated medical opinion cannot repair an unreliable factual foundation.

Before asking whether the practitioner departed from an appropriate standard, the expert must know what actually happened, or what facts are being assumed for purposes of the opinion.

Factual basis

The factual basis of an expert opinion consists of the records, information, assumptions and other evidence from which the expert's reasoning proceeds.

The practitioner should therefore ask:

  • What records did the expert receive?
  • Which facts did the expert assume?
  • Which facts were actually established?
  • Are any important records missing?
  • Does the expert distinguish fact from assumption?
  • Does the opinion change depending on disputed facts?

This is not merely a technical exercise. It may determine whether the opinion is capable of assisting the court at all.

In Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 , the Supreme Court of Appeal reiterated that an expert must lay a factual basis for conclusions and explain the reasoning supporting the opinion. The court is entitled to test whether the opinion rests on incorrect facts or assumptions.

Similarly, C.B.M v MEC for Health, Gauteng Provincial Government [2024] ZAGPPHC 562 stressed the importance of medical records and warned against expert conclusions based on mere speculation or equally feasible possibilities.

5. Standard of Care

In a medical-negligence claim, an expert may be required to explain the professional standard against which conduct should be evaluated and whether the clinical decisions under consideration were reasonably defensible.

Standard of care

The standard of care asks what level of skill and care could reasonably be expected of a practitioner in the relevant circumstances and field.

Where specialist conduct is in issue, the relevant comparison may involve the reasonable specialist in that field.

The expert's role is not simply to say: “I would have done something different.”

The useful opinion should explain:

  • what clinical circumstances existed;
  • what options were reasonably available;
  • what risks and benefits were involved;
  • what a reasonably competent practitioner would have considered;
  • what was actually done; and
  • why the conduct did or did not fall below the applicable standard.

Oppelt endorsed the established approach to professional negligence and emphasised the importance of evaluating the evidence in the context of the particular medical circumstances.

In Cheminais v Crane [2025] ZAWCHC 183 , the court reiterated the Linksfield approach: a body of professional opinion may support a course of conduct, but the opinion must still have a logical basis and withstand analysis.

6. Causation Requires a Different Question

An expert who is perfectly placed to discuss diagnosis or treatment may not necessarily be the correct expert to answer the causation question.

This is why the proposition must be identified before the expert is chosen.

Expert evidence on causation

Causation may require specialised evidence concerning the physiological mechanism of injury, the timing of injury, the effect of delayed treatment, alternative causes and the probability of a different outcome if appropriate treatment had been provided.

The practitioner should separate:

  • what caused the injury;
  • when the injury probably occurred;
  • whether the alleged breach contributed to the injury;
  • whether another cause better explains the outcome; and
  • whether earlier or different treatment would probably have changed the outcome.

These may require different experts and different opinions.

Oppelt is particularly useful on this distinction. The Constitutional Court evaluated expert evidence concerning the probability of avoiding paralysis with earlier treatment and considered whether the evidence established factual causation on a balance of probabilities.

Practitioner warning: Do not ask an expert only whether the defendant was negligent. Ask the expert to identify the medical proposition that would allow the court to decide that issue.

7. Drafting the Instruction to the Expert

The instruction letter is part of the architecture of the evidence.

A vague instruction is likely to produce a vague report.

The practitioner should therefore give the expert enough information to understand the clinical history without writing the expert's conclusion for them.

A useful instruction normally identifies:
  • the identity and background of the patient;
  • the clinical event under investigation;
  • the records supplied;
  • the questions requiring specialised opinion;
  • any factual assumptions that require testing;
  • the issues on which the practitioner specifically seeks assistance;
  • any alternative factual scenarios that may be relevant; and
  • the deadline and procedural purpose of the report.

Expert instruction

The instruction should identify the legal and factual questions without coaching the expert toward a desired answer.

A useful instruction might ask:

“Having regard to the records supplied, was the presentation consistent with the diagnosis later made?”

“What investigations would reasonably have been considered at that stage?”

“Was referral to another practitioner or facility indicated?”

“Was the treatment provided appropriate in the circumstances?”

“What, in your opinion, caused the injury?”

“If the patient had received the alternative intervention at the identified time, what outcome was medically probable?”

The expert should remain responsible for the medical reasoning.

Good instruction: identifies the proposition to be answered.

Poor instruction: tells the expert what legal conclusion the practitioner wants.

8. Give the Expert the Right Material

A practitioner should not assume that “the hospital file” is everything the expert needs.

The material supplied may include:

  • hospital records;
  • nursing notes;
  • theatre records;
  • anaesthetic records;
  • laboratory results;
  • radiology reports and images;
  • previous medical records;
  • subsequent treatment records;
  • rehabilitation records;
  • prescriptions and medication records;
  • relevant witness statements; and
  • other expert reports where appropriate.

The objective is not simply to give the expert a large bundle. It is to give the expert the material necessary to answer the particular question reliably.

Do not hide the difficult material. An adverse record, contradictory entry or previous medical condition may be critical to the reliability of the expert opinion. The expert should know about material facts that could affect the conclusion.

The importance of the factual record was emphasised in C.B.M v MEC for Health [2024] ZAGPPHC 562 , where the court stressed that medical records are crucial in medical- negligence adjudication and that speculation cannot substitute for a proper evidential foundation.

9. The Medico-Legal Report

The report should allow the court and the opposing side to understand how the expert reached the conclusion.

Medico-legal report

A useful report should identify the expert's expertise, the information considered, the factual assumptions made, the reasoning followed and the conclusions reached.

The practitioner should be able to work through the report and ask:

  • What question was the expert asked?
  • What material did the expert consider?
  • What facts were assumed?
  • What medical principles were applied?
  • What alternative explanations were considered?
  • Why was one explanation preferred?
  • How strong is the conclusion?
  • Which parts are certain, probable, possible or unresolved?

The report should not conceal uncertainty. A careful expert may properly say that the available information is insufficient for a firm conclusion.

Schneider is an important authority on the proper role and duties of expert witnesses, including the need to disclose the factual or assumed foundation of the opinion.

10. Rule 36(9) and Expert Evidence

Once litigation is underway, expert evidence becomes subject not only to evidential principles but also to procedural rules.

Rule 36(9)

Under the current Uniform Rules of Court, Rule 36(9) regulates the calling of expert witnesses and the delivery of expert summaries.

The current rule provides, in general, that where a plaintiff intends to call an expert, the notice of intention to call the expert is to be delivered within 30 days after the close of pleadings; for a defendant, the period is generally 60 days.

The summary of the expert's opinion and the reasons for that opinion is generally due within 90 days for the plaintiff and 120 days for the defendant.

The rule also expressly connects these requirements with case management, providing that the notices and summaries must in any event be delivered before the first case-management conference or as directed by the case-management judge.

Important: Treat these as current High Court rule provisions, not as universal deadlines for every forum. Magistrates' Court proceedings have their own procedural framework, and case-management directions may alter the practical timetable. Always check the current applicable rules, directives and case-management order.

The current wording and operation of Rule 36(9) are reflected in recent South African decisions, including Sithole v Road Accident Fund [2025] ZAGPPHC 437 .

11. Expert Meetings and Joint Minutes

Where both sides have experts addressing the same issue, the experts may be required to meet and identify what they agree and what remains disputed.

Joint minutes

A joint minute should reduce uncertainty, not conceal it.

It should identify clearly:

  • what the experts agree upon;
  • what they do not agree upon;
  • the reason for disagreement; and
  • whether the disagreement concerns an underlying fact, interpretation or professional opinion.

This is particularly important in a complex medical-negligence trial because an apparently broad agreement may conceal a very narrow disagreement that ultimately determines liability or causation.

In Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52 , the Supreme Court of Appeal held that joint minutes play an important case-management role and may limit the issues requiring evidence. Where a party wishes to depart from an agreement reached by experts, fair and timely warning is important.

Do not treat a joint minute as administrative paperwork. It can materially shape what remains to be proved at trial.

12. Facts Agreed by Experts and Opinions Agreed by Experts

The practitioner should distinguish carefully between an agreement about a fact and an agreement about an expert opinion.

Agreement between experts

South African authority recognises the importance of agreements reached in expert joint minutes for effective case management.

In Bee , the SCA endorsed the principle that litigants should not casually depart from matters agreed by their experts where the experts were directed to meet and prepare a joint minute.

At the same time, a court remains entitled to evaluate expert opinion. The fact that two experts agree does not transform a medical opinion into an automatic judicial finding.

The important practical distinction is:

Agreed factual proposition
May narrow what remains in dispute.
Agreed expert opinion
Is still expert evidence and must be considered within the evidential framework of the case.

This distinction is reinforced in later cases applying Bee, including L.P.M obo L.M v MEC for Health [2026] ZANWHC 92 .

13. Conflicting Experts

A medical-negligence trial may involve genuinely competing professional opinions.

The existence of disagreement does not mean that one expert is necessarily unreliable.

The real question becomes: Why should the court accept one reasoning process rather than another?

Conflicting experts

Where experts disagree, the court does not simply choose the expert it prefers because that expert appears more convincing or authoritative.

The court examines the reasoning underlying the opinions and considers them against the objective facts and the probabilities of the case.

The leading authority remains Michael v Linksfield Park Clinic .

The Constitutional Court's judgment in Oppelt likewise demonstrates that the court must evaluate the logic and evidential foundation of competing medical theories.

A 2026 example, Ngcobondwana v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape [2026] ZAECELLC 5 , reiterated that the expert's function is to assist the court, not advocate for a party, and that competing opinions must be evaluated by their reasoning and relation to the evidence.

Litigation lesson: Do not ask merely, “Whose expert is right?” Ask, “Which opinion is supported by the facts, assumptions, methodology and reasoning?”

14. The Unfavourable Expert Opinion

One of the most difficult moments in a medical-negligence practice is receiving an opinion that does not support the client's case.

The temptation is to commission another expert immediately.

That may sometimes be appropriate, but it should not be automatic.

When an opinion is unfavourable, ask:
  • Is the expert addressing the correct question?
  • Was the complete medical record available?
  • Did the expert misunderstand a factual issue?
  • Are the assumptions correct?
  • Has the expert considered the material contrary evidence?
  • Is the conclusion stronger than the reasoning supports?
  • Is the disagreement actually about law rather than medicine?

Unfavourable opinion

An unfavourable opinion is information about the case, not merely a setback.

The practitioner should test whether the opinion has exposed:

  • a factual weakness;
  • a missing record;
  • a causation problem;
  • a standard-of-care problem;
  • an incorrect expert selection; or
  • an issue that should be abandoned or reframed.

A second opinion is most useful when it addresses a clearly identifiable problem, rather than being commissioned simply because the first expert produced an inconvenient conclusion.

Practitioner discipline: The purpose of the expert process is to discover what the evidence supports, not to manufacture an opinion that supports the client.

15. When One Expert Is Not Enough

A complex injury may cross several specialised disciplines.

The manuscript specifically identifies the possibility of building an expert team rather than searching for one “master expert.”

CLINICAL EVENT ↓ WHAT HAPPENED? ↓ WHAT SHOULD HAVE HAPPENED? ↓ WHAT CAUSED THE INJURY? ↓ WHAT IS THE CURRENT CONDITION? ↓ WHAT ARE THE FUTURE CONSEQUENCES?

Depending upon the case, different questions might require input from, for example, a surgeon, anaesthetist, radiologist, neurologist, rehabilitation specialist, occupational therapist, psychologist, industrial psychologist or actuary.

The disciplines should never be selected merely because they appear in a familiar medico-legal template. Each expert should have a defined role.

Expert team

An expert team is useful where the case contains separate specialised propositions that cannot responsibly be addressed by one discipline.

A practical expert map might look like this:

Question Possible expertise
Was the surgery appropriate? Relevant surgical specialist
Was anaesthetic management appropriate? Anaesthetist
What injury occurred? Relevant diagnostic or treating specialist
When did the injury probably occur? Relevant clinical specialist
What is the functional impact? Occupational / rehabilitation expertise
What is the vocational consequence? Industrial psychology
How should future loss be quantified? Actuarial expertise

The question controls the team. The team should not be allowed to grow merely because additional reports have become customary in similar cases.

16. Preparing Experts for Trial

A strong written report does not automatically produce strong oral evidence.

The practitioner should understand the report thoroughly enough to know what the expert will be asked in cross-examination.

Before the expert enters the witness box

  • Review the report paragraph by paragraph.
  • Identify every material assumption.
  • Identify the strongest contrary evidence.
  • Know which opinions are firm and which are qualified.
  • Check the expert's understanding of the records.
  • Review the opposing expert's report.
  • Review the joint minute carefully.
  • Identify what has been agreed and what remains disputed.
  • Ensure that any material change of opinion has been dealt with procedurally.

Preparing the expert for trial

Trial preparation should not involve coaching the expert to give evidence favourable to the client. It should involve ensuring that the expert understands the evidence, the assumptions, the limits of the opinion and the areas of disagreement.

The practitioner should know the answer to three questions:

What is this expert actually proving?

What evidence supports the proposition?

What is the strongest argument against it?

The expert should be able to explain the opinion in ordinary language without losing the underlying medical reasoning.

The principle that experts assist rather than usurp the court's function is repeatedly stated in Bee and Oppelt .

17. Reading an Expert Report as a Practitioner

The practitioner should not read a medico-legal report passively.

Read it as though preparing the case for cross-examination.

Report review questions

  • Question: What exactly was the expert asked?
  • Material: What records and information were considered?
  • Facts: Which facts are proved, disputed or assumed?
  • Method: How did the expert reason from the facts to the conclusion?
  • Alternatives: What alternative explanations were considered?
  • Risk: Did the expert address comparative risks and benefits?
  • Conclusion: Is the conclusion definitive, probable, possible or provisional?
  • Limitations: What information would change the opinion?

18. A Practitioner Should Be Able to Explain the Report

There is an important practical test:

If you cannot explain what the expert is actually proving, you may not yet understand your own case.

That does not mean the lawyer must become a medical specialist. It means the legal practitioner must understand the structure of the expert evidence sufficiently to connect it to the pleaded issues.

The practitioner should therefore be able to explain:

  • what happened medically;
  • what the relevant professional standard was;
  • what departure is alleged;
  • what injury resulted;
  • how the expert says causation works;
  • which evidence supports the opinion; and
  • what remains contested.

19. The Cost of Expert Evidence

Expert evidence can become one of the most expensive components of medical-negligence litigation.

The practitioner should therefore continually ask whether each expert instruction has a defined forensic purpose.

Before incurring an expert cost

  • What proposition does this expert address?
  • Is the proposition genuinely disputed?
  • Could an existing expert answer it?
  • What records are necessary?
  • What report is actually required?
  • Is the issue liability, causation or quantum?
  • Will the opinion materially affect the litigation strategy?

The objective is not to minimise expert evidence at all costs. It is to avoid generating expensive reports that do not answer a material question.

20. The Expert Evidence Architecture

By this stage the expert evidence should fit into the larger structure of the case.

CLIENT'S STORY ↓ MEDICAL RECORDS ↓ CHRONOLOGY ↓ LEGAL ISSUES ↓ QUESTIONS REQUIRING SPECIALISED KNOWLEDGE ↓ EXPERT SELECTION ↓ INSTRUCTION ↓ REPORT ↓ OPPOSING REPORT ↓ JOINT MINUTE ↓ REMAINING DISPUTES ↓ TRIAL EVIDENCE

The expert evidence should therefore never exist as a collection of disconnected reports.

Each report should have a place within the case map.

21. The Practitioner’s Expert File

Expert file checklist

  • The specialised proposition has been identified.
  • The expert's discipline is appropriate to the proposition.
  • The expert's qualifications and experience have been considered.
  • The expert has received the relevant records.
  • Material adverse information has not been withheld.
  • The instruction identifies the questions requiring an opinion.
  • The report identifies the factual assumptions.
  • The expert explains the reasoning behind each material conclusion.
  • The expert identifies limitations and uncertainties.
  • Rule 36(9) requirements have been considered.
  • The opposing expert report has been reviewed.
  • The joint minute has been reviewed.
  • Areas of agreement and disagreement are clearly identified.
  • The expert's evidence has been integrated into the pleaded case.

22. Interactive Exercise: Choose the Expert

Scenario

A patient undergoes surgery and later develops severe neurological impairment.

The patient alleges that something went wrong during the operation. The medical records show the following:

  • the patient was admitted for a planned procedure;
  • anaesthesia was administered;
  • the operation proceeded;
  • the patient deteriorated post-operatively;
  • an emergency investigation was performed;
  • further treatment followed; and
  • the patient was left with permanent neurological impairment.

Possible experts include:

  • the surgeon;
  • the anaesthetist;
  • the neurologist;
  • the radiologist;
  • the rehabilitation specialist;
  • the occupational therapist;
  • the industrial psychologist; and
  • the actuary.

Question 1

Which expert would address whether the surgery itself was appropriately performed?

Reveal discussion

The relevant surgical discipline would ordinarily address that proposition. The exact specialty depends upon the procedure in issue. The important point is that the expert is chosen because of the question, not merely because the case involved surgery.

Question 2

Which expert would address whether the anaesthetic management was appropriate?

Reveal discussion

An anaesthetist would ordinarily be the appropriate discipline for questions concerning anaesthetic management.

Question 3

Which expert might address the nature and consequences of the neurological injury?

Reveal discussion

A neurologist or another appropriately qualified specialist may be required, depending on the exact injury and proposition being tested.

Question 4

Which experts may be required to address future functional consequences and loss of earning capacity?

Reveal discussion

The answer may involve more than one discipline. Functional consequences may require rehabilitation or occupational expertise; vocational consequences may require industrial psychology; and quantification may require actuarial expertise.

23. The Expert Question Test

Before appointing any expert, complete this sentence:

“I need this expert because the court must decide whether ______.”

Then complete a second sentence:

“The expert will assist the court by explaining ______.”

If those sentences cannot be completed clearly, the practitioner may not yet have identified the issue requiring expert evidence.

24. Leading Authorities for Further Research

  • Michael and Another v Linksfield Park Clinic (Pty) Ltd and Another [2001] ZASCA 12; 2001 (3) SA 1188 (SCA) — foundational authority on the evaluation of medical expert evidence and logical reasoning.
  • Oppelt v Head: Health, Department of Health Provincial Administration: Western Cape [2015] ZACC 33; 2016 (1) SA 325 (CC) — Constitutional Court authority concerning medical negligence, competing expert evidence and causation.
  • Schneider NO and Others v Aspeling and Another [2010] ZAWCHC 3; 2010 (5) SA 203 (WCC) — important authority on the independence, objectivity and duties of an expert witness.
  • Bee v Road Accident Fund [2018] ZASCA 52; 2018 (4) SA 366 (SCA) — important authority on expert reasoning, joint minutes and agreements between experts.
  • K.E.M obo O.M v Member of the Executive Council for Health, North West Provincial Government [2026] ZANWHC 139 — recent application of the Linksfield approach to medical expert evidence and causation.
  • L.P.M obo L.M v MEC for Health: North West Provincial Government [2026] ZANWHC 92 — recent discussion of expert objectivity, relevant expertise and logical reasoning.
  • Ngcobondwana v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape [2026] ZAECELLC 5 — recent discussion of the expert's role and evaluation of competing opinions.
Research note: The procedural discussion of Rule 36(9) reflects the current South African High Court framework identified in recent authorities. The applicable forum, case-management order and current practice directives should always be checked before relying on a procedural deadline.

25. The Practitioner’s Test

Do not appoint an expert because the case “needs an expert.”

Appoint an expert because a specific proposition requires specialised knowledge and that proposition matters to the outcome of the case.

The expert should then be given the material necessary to answer that proposition independently, objectively and logically.

The report should explain the reasoning.

The opposing opinion should be tested.

The joint minute should narrow the issues.

And before trial, the practitioner should know exactly what each expert is there to prove.

That is how expert evidence becomes part of a case rather than a collection of expensive reports.
