The Medical Chronology

A medical-negligence investigation should begin by converting the client's story into a reliable sequence of events.

Practitioner's Principle
A chronology is not merely a list of dates. It is a method of testing the factual story against the available evidence.

What Are You Trying to Build?

The chronology should allow the practitioner to see the patient's journey from the condition that existed before the relevant medical event through treatment, the alleged problem, injury, subsequent treatment and the patient's current condition.

The chronology should therefore connect:

  • the patient;
  • the pre-treatment condition;
  • the medical event;
  • the treatment;
  • the alleged problem;
  • the injury;
  • subsequent treatment;
  • the current condition; and
  • the alleged loss.

Dates Matter

Dates may become relevant to several different aspects of the investigation, including:

  • the sequence of clinical decisions
  • the development of the injury
  • causation
  • prescription
  • statutory notice
  • procedural steps
  • expert opinions

The Sequence of Clinical Decisions

Build the sequence

The practitioner should identify the clinical events in the order in which they occurred.

This means recording the relevant consultation, observation, investigation, result, decision, treatment and subsequent response.

Questions to ask

  • What happened first?
  • What information was available at that point?
  • What decision was made?
  • What happened immediately afterwards?
  • When did the next significant event occur?
The sequence should be reconstructed from the records and other evidence, not solely from memory.

The Development of the Injury

Do not start with the final condition

A useful chronology starts before the alleged negligent event. The patient's condition before treatment may become important when assessing what changed afterwards.

Capture the progression

  • Pre-existing symptoms or condition
  • Initial consultation
  • Relevant treatment
  • First indication of deterioration
  • Diagnosis or investigation of the problem
  • Subsequent treatment
  • Current condition
The chronology should show how the patient's condition changed, rather than simply recording that an injury eventually existed.

Causation

Use the chronology to test the causal story

The chronology should allow the practitioner to ask whether the timing of events supports the alleged causal connection between the medical event and the injury.

This does not mean that chronology alone proves causation. Rather, chronology identifies the factual sequence that must subsequently be tested through the appropriate evidence.

Questions for the investigation

  • When did the alleged problem first appear?
  • What happened immediately before it?
  • What happened immediately afterwards?
  • Were there other possible causes?
  • What do the subsequent medical records show?
Chronology is an investigative tool for causation; it is not a substitute for the evidence required to establish causation.

Prescription: When Does the Clock Start?

1. The basic rule

Prescription is one of the first issues that should be investigated in a medical-negligence matter. It should not be left until the factual and expert investigation is complete.

Section 11(d) of the Prescription Act 68 of 1969 generally provides a period of three years for "any other debt", unless another Act provides otherwise.

Legislation Prescription Act 68 of 1969 — Section 11(d)

Section 11(d) provides, subject to legislation providing otherwise, a three-year prescription period for any other debt.

Read section 11 on LawLibrary →

2. Medical negligence is not simply "three years from the operation"

A common mistake is to calculate three years automatically from the date of the operation, consultation, injection, procedure or other medical event.

That is not the statutory test. Section 12(1) provides that prescription generally commences when the debt becomes due.

Section 12(3) is particularly important in medical-negligence claims. A debt is not deemed to be due until the creditor has knowledge of:

  • the identity of the debtor; and
  • the facts from which the debt arises.

The section also contains a reasonable-care proviso: a creditor is deemed to have the required knowledge if it could have been acquired by exercising reasonable care.

Constitutional Court Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province

[2016] ZACC 10; 2016 (4) SA 414 (CC)

This is a leading Constitutional Court authority for prescription in a medical-negligence context. The Court considered what facts the claimant must know before prescription can begin to run.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

3. The knowledge enquiry

The practitioner should therefore avoid asking only:

The wrong question
"When did the operation take place?"

The better questions are:

The prescription questions
When did the claimant acquire knowledge of the identity of the debtor, and when did the claimant acquire knowledge of the material facts from which the debt arose?

4. What are the "facts from which the debt arises"?

This is often the difficult part of a medical-negligence prescription enquiry.

The practitioner must identify the primary facts which must be proved to establish the claim. The enquiry is therefore fact-specific.

The Supreme Court of Appeal has explained that the enquiry involves first identifying the relevant primary facts and then determining when the claimant acquired actual knowledge of those facts or should reasonably have acquired such knowledge.

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v M C

[2020] ZASCA 165

The SCA distinguished between identifying the primary facts from which the debt arises and determining when the claimant acquired knowledge of those facts.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

5. Does the patient have to know that the doctor was negligent?

No, not in the sense of knowing the legal conclusion.

The authorities distinguish between knowledge of the material facts and knowledge of the legal conclusion that those facts amount to negligence or give rise to a legally enforceable claim.

In Truter and Another v Deysel, the SCA held that section 12(3) requires knowledge of the material facts from which the debt arises, rather than knowledge of the relevant legal conclusion.

SCA Truter and Another v Deysel

[2006] ZASCA 16; 2006 (4) SA 168 (SCA)

A leading authority on commencement of prescription in a medical- negligence claim and the meaning of knowledge under section 12(3).

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

6. But medical-negligence claims have an important qualification

Although the claimant does not have to possess the legal conclusion "the doctor was negligent", the Constitutional Court in Links made it clear that professional-negligence cases require careful attention to the factual knowledge available to the claimant.

The party relying on prescription must identify the facts that the claimant was required to know and establish when the claimant had that knowledge.

In the professional-negligence context, the enquiry may therefore include whether the claimant possessed sufficient facts to have reasonable grounds for thinking that the injury was attributable to fault by medical staff and to seek further advice.

Constitutional Court Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province

The Court's reasoning is particularly important where the patient knows that an adverse outcome occurred but does not yet possess sufficient facts concerning its cause.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

7. Expert evidence does not automatically determine the prescription date

Practitioners should also avoid assuming that prescription can only begin once an expert has formally concluded that negligence occurred.

The courts have recognised that the running of prescription does not necessarily wait for an expert opinion establishing negligence. The factual knowledge enquiry must be undertaken on the evidence available to the claimant at the relevant time.

SCA WK Construction (Pty) Ltd v Moores Rowland and Others

[2022] ZASCA 44

The SCA considered the section 12(3) enquiry and observed that expert opinion evidence concerning negligence is not necessarily required before prescription can commence.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

8. Actual knowledge and deemed knowledge

Section 12(3) recognises two important routes:

  • Actual knowledge: the claimant actually knew the identity of the debtor and the relevant facts.
  • Deemed or constructive knowledge: the claimant is treated as having knowledge where it could have acquired the relevant information by exercising reasonable care.

The practitioner should therefore investigate not only what the claimant actually knew, but also whether the evidence supports an argument that the claimant reasonably could have acquired that knowledge earlier.

SCA Macleod v Kweyiya

[2013] ZASCA 28; 2013 (6) SA 1 (SCA)

The SCA considered actual and constructive knowledge under section 12(3), including the burden resting on the party relying on prescription.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

9. Who bears the burden?

In a prescription dispute, the party relying on prescription bears the onus of establishing that the claim prescribed.

In a medical-negligence matter this means the prescription defence should identify the relevant facts, the date on which the claimant had the required knowledge, and the expiry of the applicable period.

Recent High Court authority Selepe v Member of the Executive Council for Health

[2026] ZANWHC 262 — judgment delivered 30 September 2026

This recent North West High Court decision reiterates that, for a prescription defence, the party relying on prescription must establish what facts the claimant needed to know and when the claimant acquired, or is deemed to have acquired, that knowledge.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

10. Identifying the debtor is a separate enquiry

The identity of the debtor matters independently under section 12(3). A claimant may know that something went wrong medically without yet knowing which person or entity is legally responsible.

The file should therefore record when the identity of each potential debtor became known or reasonably discoverable.

This becomes particularly important where several healthcare providers, hospitals, medical practices or organs of state are involved.

Constitutional Court Mtokonya v Minister of Police

[2017] ZACC 33; 2018 (5) SA 22 (CC)

The Constitutional Court considered the identity-of-debtor requirement and the distinction between factual knowledge and legal conclusions.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

11. Claims against organs of state: a second time-sensitive regime

If the proposed defendant is an organ of state, prescription is not the only timing issue.

The Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 generally requires written notice of the intended proceedings to be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due.

This statutory notice period should be treated as a separate compliance issue. It must not be confused with the ordinary three-year prescription period.

Legislation Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002

Section 3(2)(a) generally requires the notice to be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due.

Read the Act on SAFLII →

12. Notice does not replace the prescription analysis

The practitioner should therefore maintain two separate date tracks in a state medical-negligence matter:

Track A — Prescription
Determine when the debt became due under the Prescription Act and calculate the applicable prescription period.
Track B — Statutory Notice
Determine when the debt became due and assess whether the statutory notice requirement was complied with within the applicable period.

13. The 60-day procedural period after notice

There is also a procedural timing issue under section 5(2) of the State Proceedings Act.

Generally, process instituting the proceedings may not be served before the expiry of 60 days after the statutory notice has been served, unless the organ of state repudiates liability in writing earlier.

Legislation Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 — Section 5

Section 5(2) contains the 60-day rule and its repudiation exception.

Read section 5 on SAFLII →

14. Interruption and other prescription issues

The practitioner should not stop the analysis after identifying a preliminary prescription date.

The Prescription Act also contains provisions dealing with matters that may affect completion or interruption of prescription.

  • Section 13 — certain delays in completion of prescription
  • Section 14 — interruption by acknowledgement of liability
  • Section 15 — interruption through service of process

These provisions must be investigated against the actual history of the matter.

Legislation Prescription Act 68 of 1969
Consult the Act →

15. A worked example

Consider this hypothetical sequence:

1 January 2022
Patient undergoes surgery.

January 2022
Patient experiences complications.

2022–2023
Further treatment takes place.

15 August 2023
Patient receives information giving rise to a reasonable basis for suspecting that the injury may have resulted from fault in treatment.

20 August 2023
Identity of the relevant healthcare provider is established.

It would be unsafe simply to calculate three years from 1 January 2022 because that was the operation date.

The practitioner must determine, on the actual evidence and the applicable law, when the debt became due and whether section 12(3) postpones or affects the commencement of prescription.

16. Recent illustration: M.R.M v Lenmed Health Group Limited

The 2026 judgment in M.R.M v Lenmed Health Group Limited and Others provides a useful illustration of the practical importance of identifying when the claimant acquired knowledge of the relevant debtor and facts.

High Court — 2026 M.R.M v Lenmed Health Group Limited and Others

[2026] ZAGPJHC 416

On the facts of that case, the court found that the claimant acquired the relevant knowledge when another defendant's plea identified a previously unsued doctor and described his role in the treatment. The claim against that doctor was consequently found to have prescribed three years later.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

18. Practitioner deadline checklist

The core practitioner rule
Do not write "three years from the operation" into the file without first identifying when the debt became due under section 12 and what the claimant knew, or is deemed to have known, under section 12(3).
Research hierarchy: Start with the Prescription Act itself, then consult Constitutional Court authority, then Supreme Court of Appeal authority, and then subsequent High Court decisions applying those principles.

Important: Prescription is highly fact-specific. This article is a practitioner research aid and not a substitute for checking the current legislation, pleadings, evidence and applicable case law in a live matter.

Statutory Notice

Build notice dates into the chronology

Where the proposed defendant is an organ of state, the chronology should separately record events relevant to any applicable statutory notice requirement.

Record

  • Date on which the relevant debt may have become due
  • Date on which notice was prepared
  • Date on which notice was served
  • Method of service
  • Recipient
  • Proof of service
  • Any subsequent correspondence

Procedural Steps

Chronology should include procedural events

The chronology should not stop at the patient's treatment. Important legal and investigative events should also be recorded.

  • Request for medical records
  • Receipt of medical records
  • Outstanding record requests
  • Consultation with witnesses
  • Expert referral
  • Expert opinion received
  • Statutory notice
  • Relevant correspondence
  • Institution of proceedings
A litigation chronology should allow another practitioner to understand both the medical history and the development of the file.

Expert Opinions

Experts need a reliable factual sequence

An expert should be given sufficient factual material to understand the relevant sequence of events.

The chronology can therefore become an important working document when identifying the specialised questions that require an expert opinion.

Before sending a file to an expert

  • Check the chronology against the clinical records.
  • Identify missing records.
  • Identify disputed dates.
  • Identify assumptions.
  • Identify the precise expert question.
The better the factual sequence, the clearer the specialised question presented to the expert.

Building the Chronology

A useful working chronology can contain at least the following fields:

Date
When did the event occur?

Event
What happened?

Healthcare provider
Who was involved?

Evidence
Which document, witness or other source supports the entry?

Clinical significance
Why might the event matter?

Legal significance
Could the date affect causation, prescription, notice or procedure?

Outstanding question
What remains uncertain?

The Practitioner's Test

Before moving from chronology into legal conclusions, ask:

  • What do we know?
  • How do we know it?
  • What is missing?
  • Which dates are disputed?
  • Which dates require documentary proof?
  • Which dates may have legal significance?
  • What question must an expert answer?
Closing Principle
The chronology should expose the gaps in the story as clearly as it records the events that are already established.
