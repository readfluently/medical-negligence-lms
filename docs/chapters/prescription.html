Prescription and Time Limits

Time is one of the first issues that should be investigated in a medical-negligence matter.

But the practitioner must distinguish between several different concepts that are often incorrectly described simply as "the time limit".

The essential distinction
Prescription is not necessarily calculated from the date of the medical act.

The first question is:
When did the debt become due?

The second question is:
What period of prescription applies to that debt?

The third question is:
What must be done, and by when, to protect the claim from prescription?

Three Different Time Questions

The practitioner should initially draw three separate clocks.

CLOCK 1 — WHEN DID THE DEBT BECOME DUE?

This is the commencement question under the Prescription Act.

↓

CLOCK 2 — WHEN DOES THE PRESCRIPTION PERIOD EXPIRE?

For an ordinary medical-negligence claim, this will ordinarily involve the three-year period applicable under section 11(d), subject to the statutory rules governing commencement and interruption.

↓

CLOCK 3 — ARE THERE ADDITIONAL PROCEDURAL TIME REQUIREMENTS?

In proceedings against an organ of state, the Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 introduces a separate notice requirement and a further procedural period.

Prescription Is Not the Same Thing as "Starting Litigation"

A practitioner should be careful when saying:

"You have three years to start litigation."

That statement is useful as shorthand in some conversations, but it is legally incomplete.

The Prescription Act regulates the extinction of debts by prescription. Section 11 specifies the applicable periods, while section 12 deals with when prescription begins to run.

For an ordinary delictual medical-negligence claim, section 11(d) generally provides a three-year prescription period.

The critical commencement question is therefore not simply:

"When did the operation happen?"

It is:

"When did the relevant debt become due in terms of the applicable prescription rules?"
Legislation Prescription Act 68 of 1969 — Sections 10, 11 and 12

Section 10 deals with extinction of debts by prescription. Section 11 establishes the periods of prescription. Section 12 deals with commencement of prescription.

Read the Prescription Act — Department of Justice →

The Ordinary Three-Year Rule

Section 11(d) of the Prescription Act generally provides a three-year prescription period for any other debt, unless legislation provides otherwise.

A delictual medical-negligence claim will ordinarily fall within this category.

SCA Truter and Another v Deysel

[2006] ZASCA 16; 2006 (4) SA 168 (SCA)

This is a leading authority specifically concerning the commencement of prescription in a medical-negligence claim.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

When Does the Debt Become Due?

Section 12(1) provides the general rule that prescription commences when the debt becomes due, subject to the statutory qualifications.

Section 12(3) is particularly important for medical-negligence claims. A non-contractual debt is not deemed to be due until the creditor has knowledge of:

  • the identity of the debtor; and
  • the facts from which the debt arises.

The section contains a reasonable-care proviso concerning knowledge that could have been acquired by exercising reasonable care.

SCA — Medical Negligence Truter and Another v Deysel

The SCA held that a debt is due when the creditor acquires a complete cause of action — that is, when the material set of facts necessary to institute the claim is in place.

The court rejected the proposition that prescription waits until the claimant obtains an expert opinion establishing negligence.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Section 12(3): The Knowledge Test

In a medical-negligence matter, the practitioner should record separately when the claimant acquired knowledge of:

1. The identity of the debtor

Who does the claimant say is legally responsible?

2. The material facts

What facts give rise to the alleged debt?

3. The timing of that knowledge

On what date did the claimant know those facts?

4. Reasonable-care enquiry

Could the claimant reasonably have acquired the relevant information earlier?

The Patient Does Not Have to Know the Legal Conclusion

An important distinction must be preserved.

Section 12(3) concerns knowledge of the facts from which the debt arises. It does not simply require the patient to know the legal proposition:

"The doctor was negligent."

Negligence is a legal conclusion drawn from the relevant facts.

The question is therefore not necessarily whether the patient already possessed a lawyer's or expert's final legal conclusion.

SCA Truter and Another v Deysel

The SCA expressly rejected the proposition that section 12(3) requires knowledge of the legal conclusion that known facts constitute negligence.

Read Truter on SAFLII →

Links: A Leading Constitutional Court Authority

Constitutional Court Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province

[2016] ZACC 10; 2016 (4) SA 414 (CC)

Links is an important Constitutional Court authority on prescription in a medical-negligence context and the factual knowledge required for the debt to become due.

The case demonstrates why the court must identify the particular primary facts relevant to the claim rather than applying a mechanical date-of-treatment rule.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

Identifying the Primary Facts

SCA MEC for Health, Western Cape v M C

[2020] ZASCA 165

The SCA explained that the section 12(3) enquiry involves first identifying the primary facts from which the debt arose and then determining when the claimant had actual knowledge of those facts or should reasonably have had knowledge of them.

Read the judgment on SAFLII →

When Must Legal Action Be Taken?

This question must be answered separately from the question:

"When did prescription begin?"

Once the applicable prescription period is running, the claimant must take the necessary legal steps before the debt is extinguished by prescription.

In a conventional civil action, the practitioner should pay particular attention to the service of process.

The act that interrupts prescription is not simply the practitioner's internal decision to sue, nor merely the preparation of a summons. Section 15 of the Prescription Act deals with interruption by service of process.

Legislation Prescription Act — Section 15

Section 15 regulates interruption of prescription by service of process whereby the creditor claims payment of the debt.

Consult the Prescription Act →

Do Not Confuse These Two Dates

DATE A — DEBT BECOMES DUE

This concerns the commencement of prescription.

↓

DATE B — PROCESS IS SERVED

This may interrupt prescription under section 15, provided the statutory requirements are satisfied.

Therefore:

"When did prescription start?" is not the same question as:

"When was litigation commenced in a manner capable of interrupting prescription?"

Medical-Negligence Claims Against an Organ of State

A further distinction is required where the proposed defendant is an organ of state.

The Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 introduces a statutory notice requirement in addition to the prescription regime.

Legislation Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002

Section 3(2)(a) generally requires notice of intended proceedings to be served within six months from the date on which the debt became due.

Read the Act on SAFLII →

Statutory Notice Is Not the Same Thing as Instituting Proceedings

This distinction is extremely important.

The section 3 notice is a notice of the intention to institute proceedings.

It is not itself the summons or other process that institutes the proceedings.

The Act expressly provides that, for its purposes, legal proceedings are instituted by service of process, excluding the notice.

Therefore
Six-month notice period ≠ institution of the action

The notice is an additional statutory requirement that must be dealt with before proceedings against the relevant organ of state.
Legislation Institution of Legal Proceedings against Certain Organs of State Act 40 of 2002 — Sections 1, 3 and 5
Read the Act on SAFLII →

The Six-Month Notice Clock

Where the State Organs Act applies, the practitioner should establish:

When did the debt become due?

↓

Calculate six months.

↓

Serve the section 3 notice within the applicable period.

Importantly, section 3(3) contains its own formulation for determining when a debt is regarded as due for purposes of the notice requirement. This makes the factual determination of the debt's due date important for both prescription and statutory notice.

What If the Six-Month Notice Was Not Served in Time?

Failure to serve the notice within the required period does not mean that the practitioner should simply abandon the claim.

Section 3(4) permits an application for condonation where the organ of state relies on the failure to give the required notice.

Among other requirements, the court must be satisfied that:

  • the debt has not been extinguished by prescription;
  • good cause exists for the failure; and
  • the organ of state was not unreasonably prejudiced by the failure.
Legislation State Organs Act — Section 3(4)
Read section 3 on SAFLII →

The 60-Day Rule After Statutory Notice

Section 5(2) creates another timing issue in proceedings against an organ of state.

Generally, process instituting the proceedings may not be served before the expiry of 60 days after the section 3 notice has been served.

There is an important exception where the organ of state repudiates liability in writing before the expiry of the 60-day period.

Legislation State Organs Act — Section 5(2)

Section 5(2) provides the 60-day procedural period and the written repudiation exception.

Read section 5 on SAFLII →

The Medical-Negligence Knowledge Problem

Medical-negligence cases can create difficult section 12(3) questions because the patient may know that an adverse outcome occurred without immediately knowing all the material facts underlying a possible claim.

A patient may know:

  • "Something went wrong."
  • "I suffered an injury."
  • "I had to undergo another operation."
  • "My condition became worse."

But those statements do not automatically answer the legal question of when the debt became due.

The practitioner must identify the facts relevant to the particular cause of action.

Constitutional Court Links v MEC for Health, Northern Cape Province

Links illustrates the importance of identifying the factual cause of the patient's condition when determining the relevant knowledge for prescription.

Read Links on SAFLII →

Investigations, Experts and Prescription

A practitioner should not automatically assume that prescription is postponed merely because a patient is waiting for:

  • an expert report;
  • an HPCSA investigation;
  • an internal hospital investigation; or
  • a second medical opinion.

The effect of such material is fact-specific and depends on what the claimant knew, or reasonably could have known, concerning the facts from which the debt arose.

High Court — 2026 Badenhorst and Others v MEC for Health, North West Province

[2026] ZANWHC 168

The court dealt with the factual circumstances surrounding an HPCSA investigation and the acquisition of material facts in a medical- negligence matter. The court's conclusion was based on the particular evidence before it and should not be treated as a general rule that an HPCSA investigation automatically postpones prescription.

Read Badenhorst on SAFLII →

Interruption of Prescription

Identifying the preliminary prescription date is not always the end of the analysis.

The Prescription Act contains provisions dealing with interruption and circumstances affecting completion of prescription.

  • Section 13 — certain delays in completion
  • Section 14 — interruption by acknowledgement of liability
  • Section 15 — interruption through service of process

What Happens After Judgment?

The practitioner must distinguish the original medical-negligence debt from a judgment debt.

BEFORE JUDGMENT

The practitioner is dealing with the underlying medical-negligence debt and its applicable prescription period.

↓

AFTER JUDGMENT

The resulting judgment debt falls within the judgment-debt category under section 11(a)(ii).

Section 11(a)(ii) provides a 30-year prescription period for a judgment debt.

This must not be misunderstood as meaning that a patient has 30 years in which to institute the original medical-negligence claim.

Legislation Prescription Act — Section 11(a)(ii)
Read the Prescription Act →

Section 15(4): The Fresh Prescription Period

Section 15(4) provides for prescription to commence afresh where prescription has been interrupted by service of process and judgment is subsequently given, from the day on which the judgment becomes executable.

SCA Eley (formerly Memmel) v Lynn & Main Inc

[2007] ZASCA 142; 2008 (2) SA 151 (SCA)

The SCA considered the relationship between sections 11(a)(ii) and 15(4) of the Prescription Act.

Read Eley on SAFLII →

The Practitioner’s Time-Limit Table

ORDINARY MEDICAL-NEGLIGENCE CLAIM

Question: When did the debt become due?

General prescription category: Three years under section 11(d), subject to the Act.

Action required: Protect the claim before prescription extinguishes the debt, including consideration of service of process under section 15.


CLAIM AGAINST AN ORGAN OF STATE

Question: When did the debt become due?

Additional requirement: Generally serve section 3 notice within six months.

Further procedural issue: Generally no process may be served before 60 days after the notice, subject to the statutory exception.

Prescription: Remains a separate issue and must still be addressed.


AFTER JUDGMENT

Question: What is the prescription regime applicable to the judgment debt?

General statutory period: 30 years under section 11(a)(ii).

The Practitioner's Test

Before closing the prescription investigation, ask yourself:

  • Have I identified when the debt became due?
  • Am I confusing the medical event with the prescription commencement date?
  • Have I identified the claimant's knowledge of the debtor?
  • Have I identified the material facts?
  • Have I considered the reasonable-care proviso?
  • Have I distinguished prescription from the commencement of litigation?
  • Have I checked whether the defendant is an organ of state?
  • Have I calculated the six-month statutory-notice period where applicable?
  • Have I considered the 60-day rule?
  • Have I checked whether prescription was interrupted?
  • If judgment exists, have I distinguished the judgment debt from the original medical-negligence debt?
The Core Practitioner Rule
Do not ask only:

"When did the medical event happen?"

Ask:

"When did the debt become due, what prescription period applies, what procedural requirements must be satisfied, and what must be done before the applicable deadline?"
Authority hierarchy: The Prescription Act is the starting point. Constitutional Court authorities such as Links and Mtokonya should then be considered, followed by Supreme Court of Appeal authorities such as Truter, M C and Eley. Later High Court decisions are useful for seeing how those principles are applied to particular facts.
